What the curve tells us:

Every point = one price and the quantity sellers are willing to supply at that price.

Price ↑ → slide up: an increase in quantity supplied
Price ↓ → slide down: a decrease in quantity supplied

Both are movements along the curve — the curve itself does not move. Only the price changed.

WHY ↑ REASON ① · 利润动机

Reason ① Profit — the price must cover the cost

Suppose one cup costs ¥8 to make. Sell it for less than that and the firm loses money on every cup.
Price (¥)Cost per cup (¥)Profit per cup (¥)Cups offered
108+21
208+123
308+225

Same cost, higher price → a bigger profit on every cup → the firm wants to supply more. That is the schedule you met earlier: 1 / 3 / 5 cups.

Key idea: a firm supplies a cup only if the price covers the cost of producing it. Price ↑ → profit per unit ↑ → quantity supplied ↑.

Watch the difference

Profit per cup = price − cost. If the cost rises, the same price buys less profit → the firm offers fewer cups at every price. That is a shift of the whole curve, not a movement along it.

WHY ↑ REASON ② · 成本递增

Reason ② Rising costs at higher output

Producing more is not “more of the same”: overtime pay, an extra shift, older machines kept running. The extra cost of each additional cup rises.
Cup1st2nd3rd4th5th6th
Extra cost of that cup (¥)81218243036

A firm offers a cup only while the price covers the extra cost of making it: at ¥20 it stops after the 3rd cup; at ¥30 it goes on to the 5th.

Key idea: because each extra unit costs more to make, a higher price is needed to make more output worth producing — this is why the supply curve slopes upward.

DETERMINANT ① RESOURCE COSTS · 成本

Resource costs — the biggest determinant

Anything that changes what it costs to make the good shifts the whole supply curve.

Costs FALL → shift RIGHT

= an INCREASE in supply

At every price, firms are willing to offer more.

e.g. cheaper tea leaves, lower rent, cheaper electricity, a new low-wage workforce

Costs RISE → shift LEFT

= a DECREASE in supply

At every price, firms are willing to offer less.

e.g. higher wages, dearer packaging, higher rent, more expensive energy

Why? Profit per cup = price − cost per cup. If costs rise and the price stays the same, the profit on every cup shrinks — so the shop makes fewer.

Careful with the words: “increase in supply” = the whole curve shifts. An “increase in quantity supplied” is only a movement along it.

DETERMINANT ② INDIRECT TAX · 间接税

A per-unit (excise) tax — how far does the curve move?

An indirect tax is an extra cost on every unit sold — so it moves the supply curve.

Step 1 — an extra cost

A tax raises the firm's cost per cup by the amount of the tax.

Step 2 — the whole curve moves

Sellers need a higher price by the tax at every quantity → curve UP.

How far? The gap between the two curves = the tax per unit — not the total tax bill.

TYPES OF TAX · 直接税与间接税

Direct or indirect? — only one of them moves supply

Told apart by what is taxed — and that decides which curve moves.
Direct taxIndirect tax
Taxed onincome & wealthgoods & services
Who paysthe person or firm it is levied on — it cannot be passed oncollected by the seller, who passes it on in the price
Examplesincome tax, corporation tax, inheritance taxexcise duty (fuel, alcohol, cigarettes), VAT, sales tax

Quick check — which kind is each one?

A tax on a packet of cigarettes → indirect (shifts supply). A tax on a teacher's salary → direct (no shift of supply).
A rise in corporation tax → direct: it cuts the firm's profit, but it is not a per-unit cost, so the supply curve stays where it is.

DETERMINANT ② SUBSIDY · 补贴

A subsidy — the same logic, the other way

A subsidy is money the government pays towards the cost of each unit.

Step 1 — the cost goes down

A subsidy cuts the firm's cost per cup by the amount of the subsidy.

Step 2 — the curve moves down

Sellers accept a lower price by the subsidy at every quantity → curve DOWN.

Tax: up/left · subsidy: down/right.

How far? The gap = the subsidy per unit — same rule, opposite direction.

DETERMINANT ③ THE REST · 其余

Technology, other goods, rivals — and more

Smaller determinants, all examinable — and each one shifts the whole curve.

🔧 Technology

Better machines or methods cut the cost per cup → shift RIGHT. Broken-down equipment → shift LEFT.

🌦 Weather

It works through costs: a good harvest means cheaper materials → RIGHT; a storm destroys the crop → LEFT.

🏪 Number of sellers

New shops open in the market → market supply shifts RIGHT. A shop closes → LEFT.

…and more

Price of other goods the firm could produce — a better-paying product pulls resources away → LEFT. Expectations — firms expecting a higher price hold stock back now → LEFT.

All of these move the WHOLE curve. Only a change in the price of the good itself takes us along it.

AP SKILL ① · 沿线

Move along the curve

The canteen puts the price up by ¥10. Nothing else changes — not the cost of tea, not the machines, not the number of shops.

What happened?

Only the price changed. The shop moved from 3 cups to 5 cups — up along the same curve.

Say it precisely: quantity supplied rose from 3 to 5 cups — the supply curve did not move.

“The price rose, so supply increased” is wrong. The price changed, so only quantity supplied changed.

AP SKILL ② · 平移

Supply — the whole curve shifts

Now the price stays at ¥30, but the shop's rent rises by ¥5 a cup. At every price the shop offers fewer cups — so the whole curve moves.

What happened?

A determinant changed (the rent). At every price the shop now offers fewer cups, so the curve moved up / to the left.

Key idea: the sentence does not mention the good's own price → the whole curve shifts. Here you say “supply fell”, not “quantity supplied fell”.

DETERMINANTS · 供给的决定因素

What shifts the curve — and which way?

The good's own price is not on this list. Each factor below appears once, with the direction it is usually met in.

⬅ SHIFT LEFT — a decrease in supply

Resource costs rise — wages, materials, rent, energy
An excise (indirect) tax is imposed on the good
Firms expect a higher price later, so they hold stock back
The price of other goods the firm could produce rises

➡ SHIFT RIGHT — an increase in supply

Better technology / higher productivity
A subsidy is paid on every unit
More sellers enter the market

Flip any arrow: the same factor working the other way moves the curve the other way.

WORDING · 术语对照

Wording — move along, or shift?

One pair of words moves along the curve; the other moves the whole curve. AP is strict about this.
WordingCauseOn the graph
Increase in quantity supplied — also called an extension of supplyprice ↑slide up along the same S
Decrease in quantity supplied — also called a contraction of supplyprice ↓slide down along the same S
Increase / decrease in supplya determinant changesthe whole curve shifts S → S′

Three traps, one page

① “The price rose, so supply increased.” — wrong words: nothing shifted, only quantity supplied changed.
② A tax pushes costs up → supply shifts left; the higher price that follows is only a movement along the new curve. The shift came first.
③ Supply is not the stock on the shelf — it is what sellers would offer at each price, so it is drawn as a whole curve.
