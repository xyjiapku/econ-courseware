FROM FIRMS TO GOVERNMENT · 引入

The same number, a new decision-maker

Firms use PED on prices; governments use it on taxes — PED decides who really pays.

Firms — recap 💼

Elastic demand → a price cut pays. Inelastic → pricing power.

Government 🏛️

Revenue → tax inelastic goods; discourage → tax elastic ones.

The question of the day

Coursebook: incidence = how an indirect tax is shared between consumers and firms.

Legal: the firm hands the money over. Economic: who is really worse off — shared.

Our market: $30 / 5 cups; tax $20 a unit.

STEP 1 · 供给

Start with the market — then add the tax

Market first, tax second. Watch the curve move — then the quantity.

⓪ The market before the tax

D and S cross at $30 / 5 cups — the base every later figure is measured against.

① A cost → supply shifts

$20 more to supply each unit → S up $20 → S + tax. (Sideways: 4 cups to the left.)

Demand unmoved — tastes unchanged. Only the cost of supplying changed.

② The market shrinks

New equilibrium on S + tax: 5 → 3 cups — two cups priced out.

TAX TYPES · 税种

Two families of tax

Direct taxes fall on what you earn; indirect taxes fall on what you spend — and only an indirect tax can be passed on.
Direct taxIndirect tax
Falls on income, profit, wealth spending on goods and services
Examples income tax · corporation tax · property tax · estate tax excise tax (fuel, tobacco, alcohol) · VAT · sales tax · a tariff
The diagram hits income → demand moves, but the direction is not fixed hits supply, not demand → S shifts up
Words in AP questions direct tax · tax on income · tax on wealth indirect tax · per-unit tax · tax on spending

A direct tax leaves demand unfixed: a normal good → D shifts left; an inferior good → D can shift right.

An indirect tax — a per-unit tax on a good — touches supply only, and how far it travels is the question of incidence.

STEP 2 · 价格

One price becomes two

Buyers pay more, sellers keep less. The gap between them is the tax.

① Buyers pay: $40

Was $30. Now $40 → $10 more.

② Sellers keep: $20

Of the $40: $20 to the government, $20 kept → $10 less.

The $20 gap = the tax. Two prices, one market — always say which.

STEP 3 · 收入

The government's revenue is a rectangle

Tax per unit × units still sold. Height = the tax, width = what is left of the market.

① Read the rectangle

$20 × 3 cups = $60 — all the government collects.

Taller tax → narrower market. Past about $25 revenue starts to fall.

STEP 4 · 分摊

Split the rectangle — who really pays

Cut the block at the ORIGINAL price. Above → what buyers pay extra. Below → what sellers lose.

① Consumers' share

$10 extra × 3 cups = $30 → the top half.

② Producers' share

$10 less × 3 cups = $30 → the bottom half.

Consumers $30 · 50%
Producers $30 · 50%

D and S equally steep → 50 : 50. Change D's steepness and the split moves.

WELFARE · 账本

Who pays — and where does the lost surplus go?

CED 2.8 (POL-1.A.4–A.5): a $20 tax in our market. Buyers lose, sellers lose, the government gains — and one piece disappears.

CS 62.5 → $22.5  ·  PS 62.5 → $22.5

Government 0 → +$60  ·  total $105 (from $125)

Deadweight loss 0 → $20

The accounting: $40 + $40 lost = $60 to the government + $20 to nobody.

Incidence = how the burden is split. Efficiency = how much is lost (the grey triangle).

THE RULE · 规律

The side that cannot escape pays more

What you just watched, in two lines. Supply held still throughout.

Demand INELASTIC (steep D)

Buyers cannot walk away → firms pass the tax on → consumers bear most.

Demand ELASTIC (shallow D)

Buyers walk away → firms absorb it → producers bear most.

Coursebook: "In the case of products with inelastic demand, consumers bear most of the tax… if products have elastic demand, it is the firms who bear most of the tax."

One line: the tax lands on whoever has fewer alternatives — the steeper curve. Supply elasticity works the same way (2.4).

WHO WERE THEY? · 人物

The napkin, the candidate and the tax cut

One curve, four scenes — from a restaurant table to a national argument.

1. The napkin

2. The screen

3. The axe

4. The boom

Arthur Laffer — the American economist the curve is named after; he reportedly sketched it on a napkin in 1974.

Ronald Reagan — US president 1981–89; he turned the napkin into policy, cutting the top income-tax rate from 70% to 28%.

What happened next: revenue did not rise on cue, the deficit widened — and economists still argue about what the cut actually did.
