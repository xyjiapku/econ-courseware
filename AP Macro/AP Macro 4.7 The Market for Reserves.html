Unit 4 · Lesson 11 · The market

A market you never see — and every bank lives in it

What is traded

Reserve balances — the deposits banks keep in their accounts at the Federal Reserve. Not cash in a vault, not vault money at all: a bookkeeping balance at the central bank.

Who trades

A bank holding more reserves than it needs lends overnight to a bank that is short — and is repaid the next morning.

The price

The federal funds rate: the interest on a one-night loan of reserves. It is the rate the Fed targets, and it drags every other short-term rate along with it.

Why it matters

This is the market the Fed actually operates in. When the FOMC "raises rates", this is the price it is moving.

One market, one price, settled every night by the banks themselves — the Fed only steers it.

Unit 4 · Lesson 11 · The new lever

IORB — pay banks to hold reserves

The old tools could not drain a flood this size: selling the securities back would take years, and the reserve requirement is a blunt instrument. So the Fed changed what it charged, not what it held.
\r\n

October 2008

Congress authorised the Fed to pay interest on reserve balances. First payments: December 2008. Since July 2021, one single rate: IORB.

No bank lends below what the Fed pays for waiting
The awkward years: reserves became ample in 2008 but the Fed kept describing a scarce-reserve system until January 2019 — it steered the rate with IORB the whole time. The reserve requirement itself was cut to 0% in March 2020, which is why the quantity of reserves no longer does anything.
A price set by an announced rate, not by scarcity — economists call these administered rates.
Unit 4 · Lesson 11 · Real world check

Two regimes — and who runs which

One instrument, two settings: with limited reserves the bank sets the quantity; with ample reserves it sets the price. Most advanced economies switched decades ago — the US is the newcomer.
Ample reserves — the price is the lever

No requirement (or 0%); reserves earn interest.

United States — 0% (2020), pays IORB 3.90%
United Kingdom — none since 1981, at Bank Rate
Canada · Australia · New Zealand — none
Sweden · Norway · Switzerland · euro area
Hong Kong, China
Limited reserves — the quantity is the lever

Still a live dial: raise it to drain lending.

China — 7.5%, the main liquidity dial
Turkey — 25% · Brazil — 21%
Indonesia — 9% · Russia — 8.5%
India — 3%, cut from 4% in 2025
India pays no interest on required reserves.
Advanced economies steer with prices; China, India, Brazil and Turkey steer with the quantity.

Unit 4 · Lesson 11 · Today’s prices and how the pieces fit

The administered rates, right now

Every line here is a decision, not a market outcome — that is what “administered” means.

What changed

Open market operations no longer steer the price. The Fed buys securities only to keep reserves ample; the administered rates do the steering.

What still looks the same

The announcement, the FOMC calendar, the direction language — everything from the policy lesson still applies. Only the transmission changed.

One sentence for the exam: scarce world = quantity tool; ample world = administered prices.  (Board decisions effective 17 Sep 2026 — Fed Implementation Note, 16 Sep 2026.)

Unit 4 · Lesson 11 · Worth thinking about

Money that earns money — both sides

Interest used to be the reward for investing. Now a bank can earn 3.90% by a bookkeeping entry — no loan, no risk, no production. Is that a problem?

The worry

If waiting alone pays, does lending still get its fair share? If the risk-free rate sits above growth for years, is “parking” better than building?

Someone pays

The Fed’s interest bill outgrew its bond income: it has run at a loss since September 2022, booking a deferred asset of over $200 billion — a fiscal cost of choosing this framework.

Not a gift — a price tag

IORB replaces the banks’ overnight scramble with one announced risk-free rate. Without it, trillions of reserves would sink market rates — there would be no floor at all.

Still open

A risk-free return for merely holding money is now permanent infrastructure. Does that change what banks lend for, and what “money” is for? Economists are genuinely divided.

Hold both sides: the floor makes policy work — and quietly rewrites what it means for money to earn.
