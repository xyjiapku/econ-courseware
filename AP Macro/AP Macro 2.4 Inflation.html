Review · Where we finished last lesson

GDP: three approaches, one number

GDP (Y) = C + I + G + (X − M)

Income approach

Add up all income earned — wages + rent + interest + profit.

Expenditure approach

Add up all spending on final goods.

Value-added approach

Add sales − purchases at each stage.

GDP per capita = GDP ÷ Population — and GDP misses the underground economy, home production, and says nothing about quality of life.

One blind spot we parked on purpose: GDP measured in dollars mixes up output and prices. Today we separate them.
Topic 2.6 · Real v. Nominal GDP (MEA-1.I)

Two ways to say "how big is the economy"

Nominal GDP

Values the production of goods and services at current prices.

How much is SPENT

Real GDP

Values the production of goods and services at constant prices (base-year prices).

How much is PRODUCED

Real GDP removes the effect of changes in the overall price level — so a rise in real GDP means more output, not just higher prices.
Tip: "real" means the value has been adjusted for inflation to show actual changes in volume of output. 实际 = 剔除价格变动后的产量变化

Topic 2.6 · How good is the shortcut?

Splitting nominal growth into "more stuff" and "higher prices"

Real GDP Growth Rate ≈ Nominal GDP Growth Rate − Inflation Rate
Our example: 21% nominal = 10% real + 10% inflation. Exactly: (1 + real)(1 + inflation) = 1 + nominal → 1.10 × 1.10 = 1.21 ✓

So the shortcut misses exactly one term: r × π. How big is that?

Rule of thumb: when both numbers are in single digits the error stays well under 1 percentage point — safe to use. Once either is large (hyperinflation), the shortcut breaks down and you must use the exact form.
Topic 2.6 · The GDP deflator (MEA-1.J.2)

Turning nominal GDP into real GDP

GDP deflator = Nominal GDPReal GDP × 100

Our example

726660 × 100 = 110

The price level is 10% higher than the base year.

And back again

Real GDP = Nominal GDP ÷ (deflator ÷ 100)

726 ÷ 1.10 = $660 ✓

Inflation rate = percentage change in a price index — the GDP deflator is one such index, the CPI is another. (MEA-1.F.3)
Base year check: in the base year nominal = real, so the deflator is always exactly 100.
Aim 3 · Price stability

Why "low and stable" is the target, not zero

Price stability means the price level does not change significantly — i.e. low inflation.

① Target

Often around 2% per year.

② Certainty

Firms and households can plan for the future.

③ Competitiveness

Keeps export prices attractive.

Topic 2.4 · Price indices and inflation (MEA-1.F)

Three words the exam loves to confuse

Inflation 通货膨胀

A sustained rise in the general level of prices of goods and services over a period of time.

Deflation 通货紧缩

A sustained fall in the general level of prices. Effectively negative inflation.

Disinflation 通胀放缓

A fall in the rate of inflation. Prices are still rising, just more slowly — e.g. from 10% to 7%.

Disinflation is not deflation. Prices still going up = disinflation. Prices actually falling = deflation.
Deflation · Why economists fear it more than mild inflation

The deflationary spiral

Each turn makes the next one worse — the loop feeds itself.

The debt channel

Falling prices raise the real value of debt → defaults rise → banks weaken → credit dries up.

Why 2% and not 0%

A positive inflation target leaves a buffer so a bad shock cannot tip the economy into the spiral.

Case study

Japan from the 1990s — the "lost decades" of stagnation and falling prices.

Deflation vs. disinflation · What the headlines get wrong

Two words the news uses interchangeably — they are not

Reading the news, always ask one question: are prices actually falling, or are they just rising more slowly? Falling = deflation. Rising more slowly = disinflation.
Topic 2.4 · Measuring the cost of living

Why we need a basket, not just the deflator

The inflation rate is the percentage change in the price level from the previous period.

GDP deflator

A measurement indicator of inflation — but it covers everything produced, including goods most people never buy.

Consumer price index (CPI)

Measures the overall cost of the goods and services bought by a typical consumer.

Official definition: the CPI measures the change in income a consumer would need to maintain the same standard of living under a new set of prices. (MEA-1.F.1)

Topic 2.4 · From an index to a rate

The inflation rate is a percentage change

Inflation rate = (CPIt − CPIt−1) ÷ CPIt−1 × 100

Our example

2002: (175 − 100) ÷ 100 = 75%
2003: (250 − 175) ÷ 175 = 42.9%

Real-world scale

CPI = 102.2 → inflation rate = 2.2%

An index of 102.2 already has the base year built in.

Never report the index as the inflation rate. The index is a level; the inflation rate is its percentage change.

Topic 2.6 · One line that sorts out the whole unit

What moves, what stays fixed

Nominal GDP

Changing quantities, changing prices. Both move — so you cannot tell output from inflation.

Real GDP

Changing quantities, fixed prices. Prices pinned to the base year — only output moves.

CPI's basket value

Fixed quantities, changing prices. Quantities pinned — only prices move.

Real GDP pins prices; CPI pins quantities. That single contrast explains almost every exam question on this topic.

Consumers switch toward goods that got relatively cheaper; a fixed basket misses this.

Topic 2.5 · Costs of inflation (MEA-1.H)

What does inflation actually cost?

Some costs arise from inflation whether expected or not. Others are imposed only when inflation is unexpected.

Costs whatever people expect

  • Shoe-leather costs
  • Menu costs
  • Unit-of-account costs

Costs only when UNEXPECTED

Wealth is arbitrarily redistributed between groups — the one AP requires you to know. (MEA-1.H.1)

Why "arbitrarily"? Because the redistributions are not rewards for effort or risk — they are accidents of the price level.
Topic 2.5 · Unexpected inflation (MEA-1.H.1)

Lenders and borrowers are on opposite sides

Loan contracts run over time and are written in nominal dollars — so what the repayment is worth depends on inflation.

Inflation HIGHER than expected

Borrowers gain, lenders lose. They repay in dollars that buy less than both sides expected.

Inflation LOWER than expected (or deflation)

Lenders gain, borrowers lose. The real burden of the debt is heavier.

This is why unexpected deflation is so damaging: it silently transfers wealth from borrowers to lenders and can trigger defaults.
Topic 2.5 · The interest rate that matters — the Fisher equation

From the nominal rate on a contract to the real rate you pay

The Fisher equation, named after Irving Fisher (1867–1947).

Approximate (AP uses this)

r ≈ i − π

real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation rate

Exact form

(1 + i) = (1 + r)(1 + π)

Same idea as the real/nominal GDP split — the shortcut only drops the small i × π term.

Nominal interest rate = the rate actually paid on a loan. Real interest rate = what it costs in purchasing power.
Topic 2.5 · The costs that arise whatever people expect

Shoe leather, menus, and broken yardsticks

Shoe-leather costs

High inflation makes holding money expensive, so people spend real time and effort reducing cash balances. In the German hyperinflation of 1921–23, bank staff nearly quadrupled (100,000 → 375,000) just to move money around.

Menu costs

Changing a listed price has a real cost — relabelling shelves, updating systems, reprinting menus. Inflation forces firms to do it more often.

Unit-of-account costs

Inflation makes money a less reliable measuring stick. The classic case: taxes on phantom capital gains — an asset sold for $110,000 after 10% inflation made no real profit, yet is taxed as a $10,000 gain.

All three exist whether inflation is expected or not. Only the wealth redistribution depends on being surprised.
Hyperinflation · When the yardstick breaks

What "very high inflation" does to an economy

Definition

Hyperinflation is usually defined as inflation above 50% per month — prices roughly doubling every few weeks.

Germany 1921–23

By November 1923, one U.S. dollar bought about 4.2 trillion marks. Bank employment nearly quadrupled (100,000 → 375,000) just to shuttle cash.

Zimbabwe 2008 · Venezuela 2016–

Prices redenominated repeatedly; savings wiped out; people switched to foreign currency or barter.

Gold yuan note, 1949: a single 60-billion-yuan banknote — about enough for 70 grains of rice. At this point money stops working as a unit of account and a store of value: shoe-leather and menu costs explode, contracts collapse, and output falls.

Summary 1/2 · Topics 2.6 & 2.4 — measurement

How we measure prices and output

① Real vs. nominal

Nominal uses current prices (how much is spent); real uses constant prices (how much is produced).

② The GDP deflator

Nominal ÷ real × 100; the inflation rate is the percentage change in that index. In the base year it is exactly 100.

③ The CPI

Cost of a fixed basket relative to the base year — and it overstates inflation.

④ Real variables

Real = nominal ÷ index × 100. Always deflate before comparing across years — a 20% raise can be a real pay cut.

⑤ CPI vs. deflator

CPI fixes quantities and includes imports; the deflator uses current output and excludes imports.

The shortcut real ≈ nominal − inflation is safe when both numbers are small; the error is the r × π term.
Summary 2/2 · Topics 2.5 — what inflation costs

Why any of this matters

⑥ Unexpected inflation redistributes wealth

Contracts are written in nominal dollars: inflation above expectation, borrowers win; below, lenders win.

⑦ The Fisher equation

Real rate = nominal rate − inflation (Irving Fisher, 1867–1947). The real rate is what borrowers and lenders actually care about.

⑧ Costs whatever people expect

Shoe-leather, menu, and unit-of-account costs — all grow with the inflation rate.

⑨ Extremes at both ends

Deflation risks a self-reinforcing spiral; hyperinflation destroys money's use as a yardstick.
