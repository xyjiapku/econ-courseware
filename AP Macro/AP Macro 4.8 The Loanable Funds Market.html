Unit 4 · Lesson 12 · The two rates

Two rates — and one of them is worked out from the other

The nominal rate i

The rate you are quoted: the number on the screen, not adjusted for inflation.

The real rate r

What the loan actually bought. It is posted nowhere — you work it out by taking inflation off the nominal rate.

real = nominal − actual inflation
Nominal rate iReal rate r
Where you see itA posted rate — a mortgage, a bond, a savings accountNever posted — you compute it
What sets itThe money market, steered by the FedSaving and investment — the market built below
The real rate is the one you calculate: the quoted rate minus what prices actually did.
Unit 4 · Lesson 12 · Ex ante

The same relation, run forwards

nominal = real + expected inflation
Ex post — after the fact: the inflation that actually happened. Subtract it from the quoted rate and you have the real rate (the previous page).
Ex ante — before the fact: the inflation both sides expect. Add it to the real return they want and you have the rate written into the contract.
Nobody knows the future when a loan is signed, so both sides work with expected inflation: the real return they want, plus the inflation they think is coming.
Ex ante is the version written into the contract: expected real + expected inflation.
Unit 4 · Lesson 12 · Can rates be negative?

Can the rate go below zero?

 
Quick recall. Earlier in this unit we met the liquidity trap. Was it about the nominal rate or the real rate?
The nominal rate: at zero there is nowhere left to push, so the Fed’s lever goes slack. The real rate has no floor — which is why the real rate, not the quoted one, is what actually bites on spending.
Nominal rate: 3.0%
Inflation: 2.0%
Set inflation to 0 and the two bars are the same height. Push inflation up and only one of them can follow — that is the whole point of the page.

The short real rate is set by the Fed; the long real rate is set by saving and investment. They need not agree.

Unit 4 · Lesson 12 · What shifts demand

What shifts the demand curve

1

Government borrowing ↑

A larger deficit means the government is in the queue too → demand shifts right, r rises.

2

Policy encourages investment

Any government policy that makes investing cheaper — an investment tax credit, an accelerated depreciation allowance, a subsidy — makes more projects pay → demand shifts right.

3

Optimism rises — in firms and households

Firms that expect higher returns green-light more projects; confident households borrow more for houses and cars. On this market any borrower counts → demand shifts right.

Anything that changes how much borrowers want at every given r moves the whole curve — not a slide along it. All three push demand right, so r rises. A deficit is the classic case, and the squeeze it puts on private investment gets its own name later in the course: crowding out.

Unit 4 · Lesson 12 · What shifts supply

What shifts the supply curve

1

Willingness to save ↑

Tax-advantaged retirement accounts, or a more cautious population → supply shifts right, r falls.

2

Income and wealth ↑

Richer households save more out of any income → supply shifts right.

3

Capital inflows ↑

Foreign savers buy US assets → supply shifts right, r falls.

4

Public saving falls

A bigger deficit is negative public saving → supply shifts left. The deficit can be drawn on either curve — both raise r.

More willingness to save, more income and wealth, or capital flowing in all push supply right — and r down. A bigger deficit is negative public saving, so it pulls supply left.
Unit 4 · Lesson 12 · National saving

National saving = private + public

Private saving

Households and firms. In the US this is the big positive term — but it is modest by historical standards.

Public saving

Tax revenue − government spending. Negative whenever the budget is in deficit — which it has been every year since 2001.

National saving — the two added together

S = private saving + public saving. US net national saving ≈ 0.7% of national income (Q2 2026) — near its lowest on record.

The U.S. Treasury — the world’s largest single borrower
A deficit is negative public saving: it eats out of the pool that private investment draws on.
Unit 4 · Lesson 12 · Open economy

In an open economy: I = S + NCI

Closed economy

No borrowing or lending abroad: national saving is private saving + public saving, and it must equal domestic investment.

Open economy

Investment = national saving + net capital inflow. Foreign saving fills whatever gap domestic saving leaves.

China — the world’s big saver

Saves about 42% of GDP. Saving exceeds investment, so the surplus is lent abroad — a current-account surplus of $735 bn in 2025 (3.7% of GDP). A net exporter of capital: NCI is negative.

United States — the big borrower

Saves about 17% of GDP, only 0.7% net of depreciation. Investment exceeds saving, so the gap is borrowed abroad — a current-account deficit near 3% of GDP. A net importer of capital: NCI is positive.

National saving: World Bank, gross saving % of GDP, 2025 (China 42, US 17). Current account: SAFE 2025 (China); BEA Q2 2026 (US).
Foreign capital shifts the supply curve right, holding r below where a closed US economy would put it (Unit 6 returns to this).

Unit 4 · Lesson 12 · Beyond the exam · the dollar tide

The dollar tide — a fifty-year cycle

Why it happens

When US rates rise above everyone else’s, dollars are pulled home. That is the capital inflow shifter you met on the supply page — seen from the other side of the world.

Where the tide is now

It came in three times in fifty years. Since late 2022 it has been going out: the index has slipped from 114.8 back below 100.

A rising dollar is not just an American story — it makes every dollar-denominated debt on earth heavier to service.
Unit 4 · Lesson 12 · Beyond the exam · the dollar tide

Four times the tide broke something

1980–82

The Volcker shock. Fed funds went from 6% to 20% and the index rose 50%, to a record 164.7. On 12 Aug 1982 Mexico stopped servicing $80bn of dollar debt; the region’s external debt had reached $327bn, and Latin American income per head did not recover until 1994.

1997–98

Asia. The dollar gained about 15% while the yen slid. Thailand floated the baht on 2 July 1997 and it fell 20% the same day; by January 1998 the rupiah was down 81%, the baht 56% and the won 55%. Firms that had borrowed in dollars could not repay.

2013

The taper tantrum. Not one hike — only a hint, on 22 May. The US 10-year yield went from 1.6% to 3.0%, and in four months the rupee fell 17%, the real 13%, the rupiah 11% and the lira 10%.

2022–23

The fastest hikes in forty years — +525bp in sixteen months. The index ran from 95.7 to 114.8 (Sep 2022), a 20-year high. Sri Lanka defaulted for the first time in its history; by September, 17 sovereigns traded at distressed levels, against 8 in January.

Same mechanism every time: the dollar rises, the local currency falls, and debt borrowed in dollars grows heavier in local money. Unit 6 studies the open economy properly.
