Unit 5 · Lesson 7 · Why nations fail

The same people, the same place — different rules

A controlled comparison

Hold geography, culture and history constant and change one thing: the rules. Where the rules differ, incomes differ — by 2, 3, 20 times.

What “institutions” means

Property rights, honest courts, a stable political system, and enough competition that firms cannot hide behind protection.

Acemoglu & Robinson, Why Nations Fail (2012): the mainstream explanation for long-run income differences is institutions, not geography or culture.
  • The German inner border is drawn approximately; the Korean and Nogales lines are the real ones.

Unit 5 · Lesson 7 · Lever 1 of 4

Lever 1 — physical capital per worker

k↑
capital per worker
raises y by k0.3

The catch

Diminishing returns. Doubling k raises y by only 23%; the fourth doubling buys almost nothing.

1
Public infrastructure. Roads, ports, power, broadband — the capital firms will not build alone.
2
Mobilise domestic saving. Loanable funds: saving is what finances investment.
3
A financial system people trust. If depositors hoard gold, saving never becomes capital.
4
Foreign capital and FDI. Borrowing abroad buys machines today and interest tomorrow.
Capital deepening is the fastest lever and the one that runs out first.
  • Hence the growth-accounting result: capital alone explains about half of a 2% growth rate, not all of it.
Unit 5 · Lesson 7 · Lever 2 of 4

Lever 2 — human capital per worker

E↑
skill per worker
raises y by E0.7

Why it is the big one

The exponent is 0.7 — twice as large as capital’s. A 10% rise in E lifts output per worker by about 7%.

The catch

Diminishing returns, and quality matters more than years. More years of bad schooling is not more human capital.

1
Universal basic schooling. In rich countries the government pays for the bulk of primary and secondary education — which is why schooling is a policy variable.
2
Teachers, not just buildings. Enrolment without learning does not raise E.
3
Health. Clean water and disease control raise the productivity of every hour worked.
4
Training and mobility. Skills that firms cannot capture alone are under-supplied by the market.
Human capital is the slowest reliable lever: a cohort takes twenty years to pass through school.
  • But it compounds: better-educated parents raise better-educated children, so the effect outlives the policy.
Unit 5 · Lesson 7 · Lever 3 of 4

Lever 3 — technology

A↑
total factor productivity
lifts the whole function

No ceiling

It is the only term that raises output for every k and every E — the only source of permanent growth.

The catch

A government cannot decree a discovery. It can fund the inputs, and it can speed up adoption of technology that already exists.

1
Fund basic science. Firms cannot capture the returns to research with no commercial case — so it is publicly funded or not done.
2
R&D tax credits and grants. They shift some of the risk of applied research onto the state.
3
Adoption. Most of the US productivity surge after 1995 was firms learning to use IT, not inventing it.
4
Openness. Trade and foreign investment are the fastest way to import A.
Technology is the only lever with no ceiling — and the one with the longest and least predictable lag.
Unit 5 · Lesson 7 · Lever 4 of 4

Lever 4 — the number of people

n↓
slower population growth
raises GDP per person

Two different things

Raising L (more workers) raises total GDP. Raising L faster than G lowers GDP per person.

If the target is income per head, controlling population is a growth policy.
1
Participation. Female and older workers: childcare, transport, retirement rules. (US 67.2% → 62.4%, and growth fell with it.)
2
Demographics. A falling birth rate shrinks tomorrow’s labour force but raises capital per worker today.
3
Migration. The fastest lever of all — and the most politically costly.
4
Health and longevity. Longer working lives raise L; a larger dependent population lowers output per head.
This is the lever students forget, because it is not in the “productivity” story — it is in the denominator.
  • It is also the one with a hard ceiling: the participation rate cannot pass 100%.
Unit 5 · Lesson 7 · Supply-side fiscal policy

Supply-side fiscal policy moves the supply curves

fiscal policy aimed at SRAS and LRAS
1
Private-market incentives. Income-tax cuts, investment tax credits, deregulation — they change what households and firms choose to do.
2
Public investment. Education, transport, communication networks — they raise productivity directly.
before SRAS after LRAS after
Supply-side policy works mainly through SRAS and LRAS: the same spending buys more output at a lower price level, and potential output rises.
  • AD is not assumed frozen: tax and incentive changes also alter what households and firms choose to spend. The growth claim is about capacity, not a pure demand shift.
  • Compare the tax-cut story earlier in this lesson: demand policy raises output in the short run but leaves the price level higher and capacity unchanged.
Unit 5 · Lesson 7 · The Laffer curve

Raise the tax — revenue first rises, then falls

The market: a higher rate takes more per unit — but it also sells fewer units.
Tax per unit25
At a tax of 25 you are at the peak.
In 1981 the top US income-tax rate was 70%; by 1988 it was 28%. Economists still argue about what that did to revenue.
  • The curve is hypothetical — nobody knows where the peak is for a real economy, which is why “tax cuts pay for themselves” is a claim about where you start.
Unit 5 · Lesson 7 · The story

The napkin, the candidate and the tax cut

1974
The napkin
Arthur Laffer, at a Washington restaurant, sketches a curve on a napkin: cut the rate and revenue may rise.
1980
The candidate
Ronald Reagan campaigns on the idea that taxes are too high to collect.
1981–88
The axe
The top US income-tax rate falls from 70% to 50%, then to 28% by 1988.
Since
The argument
Revenue did not visibly jump. Economists still disagree — because nobody knows where the peak is.
top rate 70% → 50% → 28%  1981 → 1988
This is why the Laffer curve is taught as a hypothesis, not a result.
  • The 1980s cut is the natural experiment — and it is still contested, because the counterfactual is unobservable.

Unit 5 · Lesson 7 · Deficits, debt, interest

Three words people mix up

Deficit — a flow

Tax revenue − (government purchases + transfers). Measured per year.

Debt — a stock

Everything still owed at a moment: the sum of every deficit ever run.

Interest — spending

Rate × debt. It sits in the budget, so paying it reopens the deficit.

  • One loop: this year’s deficit is added to the debt.
  • The debt then generates interest.
  • Interest is itself spending, so it widens the deficit again — which is why the debt grows even in a normal year.

Unit 5 · Lesson 7 · What a surplus does

What a surplus does instead

1 · Pay the debt down

US 1998–2001: public debt 47% → 33% of GDP.

2 · Save it in a fund

Norway’s oil fund; Alaska’s Permanent Fund, $86B.

3 · Send it to households

Macao, every year since 2008: MOP 10,000 in 2025.

A surplus is the same loop run backwards:
  • Less borrowing → a smaller stock → a smaller interest bill.
  • Scale note — the balance moves inside ±4% of GDP; the debt ratio only between 30% and 50%.

Unit 5 · Lesson 7 · From deficit to slower growth

From a deficit to slower growth

1
Deficit. Spending exceeds tax revenue, so the government sells bonds.
2
Borrowing. Demand for loanable funds shifts right — supply did not move.
3
The real interest rate rises, because saving must be bid away from private borrowers.
4
Crowding out. Interest-sensitive private investment falls — the short-run step.
5
Less capital per worker → lower labour productivity → potential GDP grows more slowly.
Learn the chain — not five separate facts:
  • Links 1–4 are the short run: the market clears at a higher r.
  • Link 5 is the long run — the one that turns a deficit into slower growth.
  • The lower figure is our extension: link 5 redrawn in the Phillips diagram.
