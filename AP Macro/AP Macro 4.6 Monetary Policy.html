Unit 4 · Lesson 9 · Tool 1 — open market operations (OMO)

Open market operations — click and watch

Buying (expansionary)

The Fed pays banks with new reserves: MS shifts right, and the price of money falls.

Selling (contractionary)

Reserves leave the system: MS shifts left, and the price of money rises.

Direction is the exam

Buy = expand, sell = contract. Almost every mistake here is a reversed direction.

OMO = open market operations. This is the tool that buys and sells the bonds you met in 4.5.
Unit 4 · Lesson 9 · Tool 2

The reserve requirement

How it works

Raise rr and each deposit supports fewer loans: the multiplier 1/rr shrinks and the money supply falls. Cut rr and the multiplier grows.

Why it is no longer used

It hits every bank at once and is blunt. Since March 2020 the US requirement is 0% — the tool sits in the toolkit, unused.
Reserves are now ample, so the Fed steers with interest paid on reserves instead: a new regime, covered in 4.7.

A quantity tool, like OMO — but aimed at the multiplier rather than at reserves.
Unit 4 · Lesson 9 · Tool 3

The discount rate — the backstop

What it is

The rate at which the Fed lends reserves directly to banks, against collateral.

Rarely used — always there

Buying and selling is the everyday work. The discount window is the fire exit.

It sits above the target range — which is exactly what makes it a ceiling.

Unit 4 · Lesson 9 · In practice (Fed, 17 Sep 2026)

What the Fed actually just did — and why

September 2026: a hike

With CPI back near 3.8%, the FOMC raised the target range to 3.75–4.00% and IORB to 3.90% — a contractionary move.

The path that got here

0% through 2020–21 → hikes to 5.50% by 2023 → cuts through 2024–25 → a hike again now.

Read the announcement

The Fed announces a target range; the tools pull the market rate inside it — the market answers to the floor, not the press conference.

The chart is the reaction function: inflation rises, the target line follows it up — with a lag.

Unit 4 · Lesson 9 · Limits

Why the lever cannot fix everything

1
Lags
Recognise, decide, act — and only then does spending respond. Often a year or more.
2
Expectations
If people expect inflation, they bargain for it — and it happens.
3
The zero bound
Near zero, cutting further cannot help — so the Fed buys longer-term assets (QE).
4
Political pressure
Cheap money is popular. Independence is what protects the mandate.
A powerful lever, a slow lever — and one that has to be defended.
Unit 4 · Lesson 9 · Limits, in plain words

"Pushing on a string" — the liquidity trap

Pull a string, don't push it

Expansionary policy works by pushing rates down. At zero there is nowhere left to push — the lever goes slack.

Pat Bagley, 2008 — four strings: Fed funds, TARP, discount loans, stimulus.
Year-end policy rates. Also at or below zero: Sweden −0.5%, Switzerland −0.75%.
After 2008–09, all four central banks pinned policy rates at or near zero.

The liquidity trap

At near-zero rates people and banks hoard money instead of spending it: more reserves, no more demand. That is what happened across the rich world after 2008.

What is left when the lever goes slack? QE, forward guidance — or fiscal policy. Next two pages.
Unit 4 · Lesson 9 · When the short rate runs out

Quantitative easing — buying at the long end

What it is

Large-scale buying of longer-term assets — Treasury notes and mortgage-backed securities — paid for with newly created reserves.

Why long-term

At the zero bound the short rate cannot fall further, so QE pushes long yields down — and mortgages follow.

The debate

Did it stop a deflationary spiral in 2009, or feed the 2021–22 inflation? Economists disagree.

QE is the tool you cannot see on the money market diagram: it moves the long end of the curve.

Unit 4 · Lesson 9 · Before you move on

Three ways students lose points

1
Reversing the operation
Expansionary policy buys securities — it does not sell them. Get this line right and half the questions fall.
2
Assuming instant results
The chain works with lags. Always say "in the short run" and mention the delay.
3
Drawing the wrong money market
In the scarce-reserves story MS is vertical and shifts. The ample-reserves version — flat demand, administered floor — is 4.7.
All three are direction and timing errors, not arithmetic.
