Demand ④ · From D to AD

AD vs. D — same direction, different reasons

Aggregate Demand (AD) = total spending in the whole economy: C + I + G + NX.

AD falls when the price level rises — but NOT because of substitution. There is no "other economy" to switch to!
AD = C + I + G + NX at each price level
Demand ⑤ · What moves the whole AD curve

What shifts AD — the determinants

AD shifts only when C, I, G or NX change at a given price level. A change in the price level moves you along AD — it never shifts it.

C — consumption

Disposable income · wealth · expectations · taxes

I — investment

Interest rates · profit expectations · capacity use

G — government

Government spending (fiscal policy)

NX — net exports

Foreign income · exchange rate · trade policy

Right shift = spending rises · left shift = spending falls
Supply ③ · Short-run vs. long-run aggregate supply

SRAS slopes up; LRAS is vertical

SRAS — slopes up: in the short run some prices and wages are sticky. When the PL rises while wages stay fixed:

• Real wages fall → labour is cheaper → firms expand production.
• Contracts keep other input prices sticky (materials, energy) → costs lag behind the PL.
• Some firms misread a higher PL as a higher relative price.

Same result in every story: higher PL → more output.
LRAS — vertical: in the long run prices and wages are fully flexible.

• A change in PL only changes prices — output returns to potential GDP*.
• This is monetary neutrality: money changes the price level, not real output.
• LRAS marks the natural rate of output.
AD meets the upward-sloping SRAS at E (PL*, GDP*)
Supply ④ · How sticky are prices?

Horizontal SRAS, vertical LRAS — a spectrum

SRAS can be horizontal: in some theories prices are completely rigid in the short run.

• Firms simply produce whatever is demanded at the going PL.
• Output expands at a fixed price level up to full employment.

That is the extreme Keynesian case.
SRAS + LRAS = the supply side of AD–AS (Unit 3). Shocks shift SRAS; only real shocks shift LRAS.
Rigid prices → horizontal · flexible → vertical — they cross at GDP*
How sticky are prices? — a spectrum: rigid → sticky → flexible (Keynes' extreme short run → the classical long run)
Supply ⑤ · What moves the short-run supply curve

What shifts SRAS

SRAS shifts on cost and pricing shocks — the things that change firms' costs or expectations at a given price level.
ShockSRAS
Input prices ↑ (wages, energy, materials)left
Productivity ↑right
Business taxes ↑ / subsidies ↓left
Expected inflation ↑ (wage demands)left
Cost shocks move the short-run supply curve
Supply ⑥ · What moves long-run capacity

What shifts LRAS

LRAS shifts only on real changes in capacity. Money and the price level do not shift it — that is monetary neutrality.
Real changeLRAS
More / better labour (population, skills)right
More capital (machines, infrastructure)right
Technology improvesright
Productivity ↑right
Natural resources found / lostright / left
Capacity grows → potential output GDP* rises (economic growth)
Growth · Three different shifts

Three ways output can rise

"Growth" is not one event. Actual output rises when AD or SRAS shifts right; potential output rises only when LRAS shifts right.
① and ② are actual growth (short run) · ③ is potential growth (long run)
Equilibrium · Short run, then long run

Short-run equilibrium, then the long run

AD meets SRAS at E → the price level and output Y.
Y < Y* = recessionary gap · Y > Y* = inflationary gap.
Recessionary gap. Unemployment high → wages fall → costs fall → SRAS shifts right → Y returns to Y*, PL falls.
Inflationary gap. Labour scarce → wages rise → costs rise → SRAS shifts left → Y returns to Y*, PL rises.
Long-run equilibrium. AD, SRAS and LRAS meet at Y*. Money is neutral: it moves PL, not real output.

Why cycles happen · Keynes VS Hayek

Why do cycles exist at all?

A cycle needs three links at once: something has to move → output has to move first → and coming back has to take time.

① The spark — what moves

A shock has to hit the economy. On the demand side: confidence, investment plans, credit conditions, fiscal and monetary policy, and foreign demand — all shift AD.
John Maynard Keynes
1883–1946 · demand side
Animal spirits — instincts and emotions drive behaviour, measured by consumer confidence.
Confidence moves AD: optimism → spending ↑ → expansion; pessimism → spending ↓ → recession.
Diagnosis: too little demand. Cure: stimulate spending.
VS
Friedrich Hayek
1899–1992 · Nobel 1974 · supply side
Credit too cheap — interest rates below the natural rate → firms start long, roundabout projects.
Malinvestment: a false price signal. When credit stops expanding, the bust is the correction.
Diagnosis: a distorted production structure. Cure: let it reallocate.
② Why output moves first. Prices and wages are sticky. A shock therefore shows up as a change in output and jobs, not as an instant change in prices — that is exactly why the gap opens.
③ Why coming back takes a whole cycle. Wages are renegotiated slowly, so the return to potential is gradual — and by the time SRAS has shifted back, the next shock has usually arrived.
Put together: repeated shocks (①) + sticky prices (②) + slow wage adjustment (③) = output swinging around a rising trend, one stage after another.
Shocks · When the supply side moves

Supply shocks — and the way back to long-run equilibrium

The shock moves SRAS; the same wage mechanism then brings the economy home

Adverse (negative) shock

Input prices ↑ (oil, energy, wages) or productivity ↓ → SRAS shifts left → PL rises while output falls: stagflation. Output is now below Y* → unemployment high → wages fall → SRAS shifts right → back to long-run equilibrium.

Favourable (positive) shock

Input prices ↓, better technology, higher productivity → SRAS shifts right → PL falls while output rises. Output is now above Y* → labour scarce → wages rise → SRAS shifts left → back to long-run equilibrium.
