Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · before 1936

Before Keynes: the classical view

Laissez-faire

Markets clear on their own. Government should stand back, and budgets should balance.

Say’s Law

Supply creates its own demand — a general glut of goods is impossible.

Downturns are temporary

Falling wages and prices restore full employment — in the long run.

“This long run is a misleading guide to current affairs. In the long run we are all dead.” — Keynes, A Tract on Monetary Reform (1923)
Then 1929 came: years of mass unemployment that wages and prices did not clear — and the classical prescription was to wait.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · 1936

Keynes: what he wrote about prices

1
What the book builds
Effective demand, an underemployment equilibrium, and a case for fiscal policy.
2
What it does not build
A stable unemployment–inflation trade-off. The price level was institutional — set by wage bargaining.
Keynes is called the father of macroeconomics — the General Theory turned it into a subject of its own. Friedman (1977) said the trade-off idea “filled a gap in Keynes’s theoretical structure”; Keynes had already said the relation moves.
John Maynard Keynes
“An increase in effective demand will … spend itself partly in increasing the quantity of employment and partly in raising the level of prices … we have in fact a condition of prices rising gradually as employment increases.” — General Theory, p. 296: the relation is not stable.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · 1958

Phillips: a scatter, not a menu

The paper

Unemployment and money wage rates, UK 1861–1957 — Economica, November 1958.

What he plotted

Wage growth, Δw / w — not inflation. His curve fits 1861–1913; the post-1948 points sit to the right of it.

What he did about that

Called it a time lag, then re-plotted unemployment lagged seven months to pull the points back onto the curve.

A. W. Phillips
The MONIAC, 1949
His own words about the paper he never followed up: “a very crude attempt”, “just done in a weekend”. He was not selling a law.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · 1960

Samuelson and Solow: the curve becomes a policy tool

Two moves, one paper

US data, and the axis swapped from wages to the price level — so the same shape now reads as inflation against unemployment.

The caveat, p. 193

“It would be wrong to think that our Figure 2 menu … will maintain its same shape in the longer run.” They sketched a low-pressure economy shifting it down, or structural unemployment shifting it up.

Paul A. Samuelson
What everyone remembers: “the menu of choice between different degrees of unemployment and price stability” (p. 192) — two labelled points, 5.5% unemployment with stable prices, or 3% with 4.5% inflation.
They wrote the menu and the warning, in the same paper. The profession kept the menu.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · 1967–68

Phelps and Friedman: expectations, and the natural rate

1
Phelps, 1967
Phillips Curves, Expectations of Inflation and Optimal Unemployment over Time: workers and firms learn, so the trade-off must be written in real terms.
2
Friedman, 1968 — the AEA address
There is “always a temporary trade-off” — and none in the long run.

The natural rate, in one line

The rate “ground out by the Walrasian system of general equilibrium equations”. Inflation moves unemployment only while it is a surprise: π = πe − 1.25(u − u*).

Milton Friedman
Edmund S. Phelps
They said it before the break, and the break came roughly as described. Push u below u* and what you buy is not a lower rate — it is accelerating inflation.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · discussion

So who caused the stagflation? Two suspects.

Suspect 1 — the oil shock

SRAS shifts left, so output falls while prices rise — that is why π and u jumped together in 1974 and 1979. No demand story can do that. But one shock shifts the curve only once.

Suspect 2 — policy and expectations

Each time unemployment rose, policy eased again, so inflation never fell back to where it started. πe ratcheted up and the SRPC kept shifting. That is what turns a bad year into a lost decade.

They are not rivals: oil was the trigger, accommodation is why it persisted. Ask what you are explaining — the first jump, or why the curve never came back.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · the evidence

What the data says: a ratchet, not a spike

Already 5.4% in 1969

That is before any oil shock. And after 1974 inflation fell only to 5.8% — not back to 1.6%. The real rate was negative in 1974–75 and 1979–80.

One oil shock, four central banks

1973–82 average inflation: W. Germany 5.2%, US 8.8%, UK 14.2%, Italy 16.3%. Same oil, different inflation.

Volcker’s 1980–82 tightening broke the inflation (13.5% → 3.2%) at the cost of 9.7% unemployment. Tight money could not abolish the oil — but it could end the inflation.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · 1976

Lucas: the critique that ended the easy version

1
People read the rule
Rational expectations (Muth, 1961): agents use the same model, and they learn.
2
The critique
Lucas (1976), Econometric Policy Evaluation: A Critique — a relation estimated under one policy rule is not structural.
3
The curve was the example
The 1961–69 fit was a policy-regime correlation, so it could not survive the regime changing.
4
The policy conclusion
The new classical school — Lucas, Sargent, Barro, Prescott — added it up: if the rule is anticipated, only surprises move output.
Robert E. Lucas, Jr.
“Rules rather than discretion” — the policy slogan it produced.
The answer was not “the data is wrong” but “our equations were”. Macroeconomics rebuilt itself on microfoundations — the road to 2026.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · history of thought

Ricardian equivalence: the fiscal twin of the same critique

David Ricardo · 1772–1823

His Principles (1817) put the argument in writing — and then doubted it.

Fund a war with £20m at once, £1m a year for ever, or £1.2m for 45 years — “in point of economy, there is no real difference in either of the modes.”

Then he doubted it: taxpayers, he wrote, “do not manage their private affairs accordingly”. A benchmark, not a forecast.

Robert Barro · b. 1944

With Lucas and Sargent, a founder of new classical macroeconomics.

1974, Journal of Political Economy: “Are Government Bonds Net Wealth?” A bond-financed tax cut is only a future tax — not wealth, so AD does not move.

The name is not his: Buchanan’s 1976 comment coined “Ricardian equivalence” — and Buchanan was a critic of it.
The fiscal twin of the Lucas critique: a systematic move is anticipated and priced in, so it cannot move output.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · history of thought

The rebuild: credibility, rules — and the wave that followed

Volcker 1979–82: inflation 13.5% → 3.2%, at the price of 9.7% unemployment.
1
Credibility became the instrument
The Fed accepted a deep recession to break expectations — and it worked.
2
The theory was rebuilt
Calvo/Taylor staggered pricing: the short-run curve got explicit microfoundations.
3
Institutions followed
Inflation targeting (NZ 1990) and independent central banks anchor πe directly.
4
And the same logic on the fiscal side
The page before: a deficit is a tax moved into the future, so it is not new wealth — and cannot move AD.
This wave is the backbone of neoliberalism: if systematic policy cannot move output, the case for rules, targets and independent central banks wins.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · 12 October 2026 · the prize

The Nobel for the microfoundations

Robert E. Hall (b. 1943, Stanford) and Michael Woodford (b. 1955, Columbia) — “for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation”.

Hall: the household

Consumption as an intertemporal choice (1978). Households smooth spending, so a known future income change moves today’s spending before the money arrives.

Woodford: the firm

Prices are written down in advance, so what the central bank says about the future is already policy today (Interest and Prices, 2003).

Robert E. Hall
Michael Woodford
The new Keynesian answer to Lucas: keep the Keynesian short run, but rebuild it on forward-looking households and firms.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · 12 October 2026 · the official history

The story the Nobel tells

“During the oil crises of the 1970s, it seemed that the Keynesian models … no longer worked … Many people believed that stabilisation policy had done more harm than good and that it should be retired.” — Nobel press release, 12 October 2026

What it assumes

That policy had been run off a stable curve, that the 1970s exposed it, and that the rebuild was a lesson learned.

What it does not claim

Nothing about who believed what. It is a summary of the models, not a reading of the archive.

Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · the revisionist account

The other telling: a myth?

James Forder, Macroeconomics and the Phillips Curve Myth (2014): the standard story is “entirely inauthentic. It is made up.”
The story as it is toldWhat the record shows
Phillips discovered a striking trade-offNot new in 1958
Samuelson–Solow sold it as a menuHardly propounded a trade-off at all
Governments inflated to buy jobsRarely mentioned in policy discussion
Friedman and Phelps added expectationsExpectations were commonplace before 1968
The 1970s exposed the naive KeynesiansOil and commodity prices are overlooked
Why it matters: this is the story that discredited the Keynesians of the 1970s — and with them the case for expansionary policy.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · the L-shaped supply curve

The L-shaped supply curve — and the Phillips curve it implies

Before 1958 the AS curve was drawn as an L: flat in a recession, rising, then vertical at full employment. Forder: wages and prices were treated as exogenous — “no fundamental reason that full employment in and of itself should generate a change in the price level”.
AD-AS: flat, then rising, then the wall
Three stages, three branches
Policy stimulus → AD
Recession
Read the right panel: in the recession stage inflation does not move with unemployment at all; the middle stage is the short-run trade-off you already know; and once employment is full you get inflation with no extra output. Summers (1991) from the other side: the L “was falsified by the coincidence of inflation and less than full employment in the late 1940s and 1950s”.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Beyond the exam · how to read a history of thought

So which story is true?

Not in dispute

The 1961–69 relation, the break after 1970, stagflation, the vertical LRPC at the resting point π = πe, the Lucas critique, microfoundations, the 2026 prize.

In dispute

Who believed what, who acted on it — and whether the break was a profession’s mistake or a story told afterwards.

1
One decade, two histories
The same events carry both readings — which one you were taught is itself evidence about who won.
2
A right model can have a wrong origin story
The vertical LRPC is still the right device, and the 1960s line really had no long run in it at all.
3
And that is the point of this deck
Models come with a history attached. Ask who read the archives.
The standard account is a usable history — it organises the models. That is not the same thing as a verified history.
