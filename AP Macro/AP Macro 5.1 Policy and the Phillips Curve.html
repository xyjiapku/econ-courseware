The two toolboxes you already own

Both toolboxes do the same thing to this diagram: AD shifts right.

Fiscal policy (Unit 3)

Higher spending on roads, schools or transfers, or lower taxes.

The package is multiplied: $1 of spending moves AD by more than $1.

Monetary policy (Unit 4)

Lowering the target range so IORB falls, plus asset purchases.

The rate falls → investment and durables rise → AD right.

Fiscal − · Monetary −Spending cuts or tax rises plus rate rises — the 2022–23 slowdownleft ↓↓downdown
Same direction → AD, output and the price level all move predictably. So why do the two tools so often point opposite ways in practice?

Two different decision-makers

The Fed is independent and answers to its inflation mandate; the government answers to voters and carries the debt.

Two different clocks

A budget passes once a year and is hard to reverse; the Fed meets eight times. By the time inflation arrives, the spending is already law.

2022–23 is the textbook case: the Fed went to 5.25–5.50% while the deficit stayed near 5–6% of GDP. Tight money, loose fiscal.

Unit 5 · Lesson 1 · Real world

2020 versus 2022–26: both tools, opposite directions

2020 — double expansion

Emergency fiscal packages plus a cut to 0–0.25% and quantitative easing. Output recovered fast; inflation followed with a lag.

2022–23 — double contraction

The Fed raised the target range to 5.25–5.50% in sixteen months while pandemic fiscal support expired.

2026 — a mixed stance

Policy rate back at 3.75–4.00% with CPI near 3.8%, while the federal deficit is still 5.8% of GDP: tight money, loose fiscal.

The Eccles Building — where one half of the mix is decided
The mix matters: two tools can pull in opposite directions at the same time.

Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Already yours · demand and supply

Why is there a trade-off at all?

Step 1 — more output means more jobs

“Moving along the SRAS curve… employment must rise… unemployment will fall”. That is the trade-off.

1
Sticky wages
Fixed by contracts, so a higher price level cuts the real wage.
2
Sticky prices
Menu costs: a firm that has not re-priced sees its relative price rise.
3
Misperceptions
Suppliers mistake a general price rise for a rise in their own price.
Press Next step to build the answer.
The exam names the first two (“upward-sloping because of sticky wages and prices”); Mankiw adds the third. And the economy only ever moves along the curve or the curve itself shifts.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Real data I · FRED / BLS, 1961–69

The trade-off was there

Year: —

Nine years of American data

One dot per year, 1961–1969: unemployment on the x-axis, CPI inflation on the y-axis.

And a line fits them

The best-fitting straight line slopes down: π = 7.84 − 1.15u, with R² = 0.69. Lower unemployment really did come with higher inflation.

Mankiw’s Tenth Principle

“Society faces a _________ trade-off between inflation and unemployment.”

Nine years is not proof, but it is what a menu looks like: more inflation buys less unemployment. One word of that principle is still missing.

“Society faces a short-run trade-off…” — the missing word is in the short run: wages catch up, SRAS shifts left, unemployment returns to u*. Every resting point has u = 5%, which is exactly why the LRPC is vertical.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · The SRPC and expectations · new

What exactly moves the SRPC? Expected inflation

Expected inflation is the intercept

The SRPC is π = πe − 1.25(u − 5). Change πe and the whole curve moves up or down by that much — it does not tilt or slide sideways.

Along vs. shift

Actual inflation changes with πe fixed → move along. πe itself changes → the SRPC shifts.

Which is why the LRPC is vertical

Every SRPC crosses the LRPC at u = 5%, so the LRPC is the set of all the long-run resting points.

The 1970s were not a move along the old curve — πe rose, so the whole curve went up. That is the difference between a bad year and a broken model.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · foundations

Why is expected inflation the intercept?

1
Wages are fixed in advance
A contract sets W = w̄ × Pe — so expected inflation is already inside the wage.
2
Firms price over that wage
If P ends up above Pe, the real wage fell without anyone agreeing — so firms hire more.
3
Only the surprise moves u
u − u* = −(π − πe)/1.25  ⇒  at u = u* the only possible π is πe.
πe sits in the intercept because it is already inside the wage that was signed. The slope comes from how strongly hiring responds to a real-wage surprise — so change the rigidity and the line tilts; change πe and the whole line moves.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2

Long-run equilibrium, the two gaps, and demand shocks

The intersection

“Long-run equilibrium corresponds to the intersection of the SRPC and the LRPC”: u equals the natural rate and actual inflation equals expected.

The two gaps

Left: u below u* — inflationary gap. Right: u above u* — recessionary gap.

Demand shocks

AD right → up and to the left along the SRPC. The curve itself does not move, because πe has not changed yet.

Only a change in the natural rate of unemployment shifts the LRPC itself — longer job search, a higher minimum wage, stronger unions, or skills that no longer match the jobs.
Unit 5 · Lesson 2 · Real data III

Supply shocks: the whole curve shifts — and here is the oil

An adverse supply shock

Oil spikes, SRAS shifts left, so output falls and prices rise together — the whole SRPC shifts up. That combination is stagflation.

The evidence, 1973–1982

Oil went from $3.87 (1973) to $37.38 (1980) — ×9.7. Inflation hit 11.0% (1974) and 13.5% (1980) while unemployment reached 8.5% (1975) and 9.7% (1982).

A favourable shock does the reverse: SRAS right, prices down, SRPC shifts down. Oil fell to $15 by 1986 — but was oil the whole story?
