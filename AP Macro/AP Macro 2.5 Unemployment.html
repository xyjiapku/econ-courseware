Topic 2.3 · Who is counted as unemployed? (MEA-1.C)

Four tests, and three nested boxes

① Working age

In the working-age population — old enough to work, and not too old (not retired).

② Not institutionalized

Not in jail, hospital, the military, or full-time schooling.

③ Available for work

Able and willing — economically active. This is about whether you could start.

④ Actively searched — recently

Not just willing: you must have taken concrete steps to find a job in the recent past (the official window is the last four weeks).

    Topic 2.3 · What moves the participation rate

    Why 65% is not a constant

    Wages on offer

    Higher pay pulls people into the labour force.

    Social attitudes

    Attitudes to working women, and to disabled people working.

    Care provision

    Childcare and elderly care make work possible.

    Education

    The share of school leavers going into higher education.

    These change the size of the labour force — and therefore the denominator of the unemployment rate.
    Topic 2.3 · How unemployment is measured, and what it misses (MEA-1.D)

    Two ways to count — and both undercount

    ① Household survey

    Ask a sample of adults directly whether they are working or looking — the ILO / labour-force measure the U.S. unemployment rate is built on.

    ② Benefit count

    Count people claiming unemployment-related benefits — the claimant count. Cheap and fast, but misses those not eligible or not claiming.

    Why the official rate understates joblessness

    • Discouraged workers — want a job but stopped looking, so they are not in the labour force at all.
    • Involuntary part-time workers — want full-time but counted as fully employed.
    Neither measure captures job quality or skills mismatch. The rate is widely criticised for understating true joblessness. (MEA-1.D.1)
    Bridge · Not all unemployment is the same problem

    Before we sort them: full employment is not zero

    Some joblessness is healthy — an economy needs people in transit between jobs. We never aim for 0%.
    Frictional

    In transit between jobs; skills transferable.

    Structural

    Skills obsolete; those jobs are gone.

    Cyclical

    Caused by the downturn — and the only one the business cycle explains.

    Three different diagnoses, three different cures. Getting them mixed up is the classic exam trap.
    Topic 2.3 · Types of unemployment ① (MEA-1.E.1)

    Frictional unemployment

    Temporary unemployment or being between jobs

    Qualified workers with transferable skills — not unemployable, just in transit.

    Why it is healthy

    Matching workers to jobs takes time. A little frictional unemployment lets people find a better match.

    Examples

    A new graduate's first job search; someone who quit to find a better-fitting role.

    Short duration, skills intact, jobs exist — the person just has not met the employer yet.
    Topic 2.3 · Types of unemployment ② (MEA-1.E.1)

    Structural unemployment

    Changes in the economy make some skills obsolete

    These workers do not have transferable skills, and these jobs will never come back.

    The fix is different

    Not "wait it out" — workers must learn new skills or move to where the jobs are.

    Examples

    Automation replacing assembly-line roles; a region's main industry closing.

    Frictional = skills transferable, jobs exist. Structural = skills obsolete, jobs gone.
    Topic 2.3 · Types of unemployment ③ · the link into 2.7 (MEA-1.E.3)

    Cyclical unemployment

    Cyclical unemployment is the deviation of the actual unemployment rate from the natural rate.

    In a downturn

    Actual above natural → positive cyclical unemployment.

    In a boom

    Actual below natural → negative cyclical unemployment.

    Is it "caused by deficient AD"?

    That is the usual cause — in a downturn, weak aggregate demand means firms need fewer workers. But AP's definition (MEA-1.E.3) is broader: cyclical unemployment is simply the deviation from the natural rate, whatever the shock — demand or supply.

    Use the CED definition in the exam; use "AD fell" as the explanation. Frictional and structural exist all the time; cyclical comes and goes with the business cycle.
    Topic 2.3 · Summary — then we cross into 2.7

    Natural rate = frictional + structural

    Natural rate of unemployment = frictional + structural

    Full employment

    The rate that exists at full-employment real output — not zero. (MEA-1.E.2)

    It can drift

    The natural rate changes gradually with labour force characteristics. (MEA-1.E.4)

    Actual rate − natural rate = cyclical unemployment. That subtraction is the door to the business cycle.

    Topic 2.7 · The business cycle (MEA-2.A)

    Output rising and falling around a long-run trend

    Dashed = long-run trend, blue = actual GDP, grey bands = recessions. Cycles are fluctuations in aggregate output and employment from changes in AS and/or AD. (MEA-2.A.1)
    Topic 2.7 · Phases and turning points (MEA-2.A.2, 2.A.3)

    Two phases, two turning points

    Phases

    Expansion — output and employment rising.
    Recession — output and employment falling.

    Turning points

    Peak — the top, where expansion ends.
    Trough — the bottom, where recession ends.

    Keep the pairs straight

    A phase is a stretch of time; a turning point is the instant the direction flips. "Peak" is not the same word as "recession".

    The trend line keeps rising: cycles are short-run fluctuations around growth, not a reversal of it.
    Topic 2.7 · Output gap and potential output (MEA-2.A.4, 2.A.5)

    Actual output vs. what the economy could produce

    Output gap = actual output − potential output

    Negative gap · recessionary

    Actual below potential. Resources idle, cyclical unemployment positive.

    Positive gap · inflationary

    Actual above potential. Overheating builds inflation pressure.

    Potential output is full-employment output — the level of GDP where unemployment equals the natural rate.
    At potential output, cyclical unemployment is zero — only frictional and structural remain.
    Behind the number · How is potential output actually estimated?

    Potential output is not observed — it is built

    Nobody can measure "what the economy could produce". Statistical agencies estimate it from a production function.

    Y = A × f(K, L) output = technology × (capital, labour)

    ① Feed in the trend inputs, not the actual ones

    Capital K (stock of plant & equipment), Labour L (hours at a sustainable rate), and total factor productivity A are each smoothed to remove the cycle, then plugged in.

    Capital KLabour LTFP APotential Y

    Why cycles happen · Keynes and "animal spirits"

    Confidence is not a detail — it shifts AD

    Animal spirits

    The instincts, proclivities and emotions that influence behaviour — measured in practice by consumer confidence. John Maynard Keynes (1883–1946)

    Optimism

    Households and firms spend and invest → AD rises → expansion.

    Pessimism

    Spending and investment cut → AD falls → recession, rising cyclical unemployment.

    Overheating → stagflation → recession: a boom past potential builds inflation, and the eventual squeeze brings the downturn.

    Summary 1/2 · Topic 2.3 — unemployment

    Measuring the labour market

    ① Who is counted

    Working age but not too old, not institutionalized, able and willing, and actively seeking — miss one and you leave the labour force.

    ② The two rates

    Unemployment rate uses the labour force as denominator; participation rate uses the working-age population.

    ③ How it is measured, and what it misses

    Household survey or benefit count; both understate joblessness (discouraged workers, involuntary part-timers).

    ④ Frictional vs. structural

    Frictional: in transit, skills transferable. Structural: skills obsolete, jobs gone — needs retraining.

    Summary 2/2 · Topic 2.7 — the business cycle

    Why the labour market swings

    ⑤ Cyclical bridges the two topics

    Actual − natural — positive in recession, zero at potential, negative in a boom.

    ⑥ Phases and turning points

    Phases = expansion / recession; turning points = peak / trough. A cycle is a fluctuation around a rising trend.

    ⑦ Output gap

    Actual − potential. Potential output is full-employment output, estimated from a production function with trend K, L and productivity.

    ⑧ The one-line link

    At potential output, unemployment equals the natural rate — so cyclical unemployment is zero.

    Bigger picture · Every indicator has a blind spot

    Three goals, three imperfect yardsticks

    ① Growth → Real GDP

    Misses home production, the underground economy, leisure and environmental damage — and says nothing about how income is distributed.

    ② Jobs → Unemployment rate

    Excludes discouraged workers and involuntary part-timers, so it can understate real joblessness.

    ③ Prices → CPI

    Substitution bias, late entry of new goods, unmeasured quality — and one number cannot match your basket.

    Goodhart's Law

    “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

    Charles Goodhart, 1975

    Announce a target for the unemployment rate and people start managing the number rather than the problem behind it. The same logic hits exam pass rates and crime clear-up rates: hit the metric, miss the point.

    These are still the best yardsticks we have — just never read a single number as the whole story.
