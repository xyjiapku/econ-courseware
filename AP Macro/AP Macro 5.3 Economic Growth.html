Unit 5 · Lesson 6 · Definition and measurement

What growth is — and how it is measured

economic growth = a sustained increase in real GDP
that is the standard definition — more output, not merely more money
Growth of real GDP4.0% a year
Growth of population1.0% a year

But how is it measured?

The course measures it per person: the growth rate of real GDP per capita. A 4% rise in output with 4% more people leaves the average person no better off.

Why real, not nominal?

Prices rise. Only real GDP separates more output from a higher price level.

Output grows 4.0% while population grows 1.0% — per person, about 3.0% a year.

Unit 5 · Lesson 6 · Real data · World Bank WDI

Who actually grows the world economy

China United States India Rest of the world
Over 30 years China supplied 30% of all the growth in world real GDP — the United States 21%.
  • China’s share peaked in 2005–2015 at 36%; since 2015 it has been about a third, and the US share has risen again to 21%.
  • Real GDP, constant 2015 US$ (World Bank). Measured at PPP the Chinese share is lower (≈22%) because its price level is lower — the direction of the story does not change.

Factor two can move only once

In 1941–44, millions of American women entered paid work: the share of adults employed outside the home rose from 50% to 58%. Output per person jumped — then the effect was used up.

And it is now falling

US labour-force participation peaked at 67.2% in 2000 and fell to 62.4% by 2015. Growth of real GDP per capita fell with it, from 2.2% a year before 2007 to 1.6%.

1
There is a ceiling of 100%. Participation cannot keep rising, so any growth it delivers is a one-time level shift, not a rate.
2
Output per worker has no such ceiling. The same person with better machines, more schooling and better methods can go on producing more, year after year.
3
So the long-run rate is the productivity rate. Over decades the second factor drifts, but it cannot be the engine.
Productivity = real GDP ÷ number of people working — output per worker.
  • Everything that follows is about this one number: what raises it, and what policy can do about it.
Unit 5 · Lesson 6 · Three determinants

Where productivity comes from

1 · Physical capital

Machines, buildings, tools. A worker with a backhoe moves more earth than one with a shovel.

2 · Human capital

Skill embodied in people. In 1910, 13.5% of US adults had finished high school and 3% had a degree; by 2020, 90% and 36%.

3 · Technology

The technical means of production. Not only chips: the paper bag (1870) and the barcode (1970s) moved productivity too.

Same worker, same hours — a 7× difference between the US and India.
  • Everything in this deck works through these three channels: K/L, H/L, and A.
Unit 5 · Lesson 6 · The production function

One function, three properties we actually observe

Y = A · Kα · L1−α
α = 0.3  ·  A = technology, K = capital, L = labour
1
Constant returns to scale. Double K and L together and output doubles exactly — no gain from size alone.
2
Diminishing returns to each input. Because 0 < α < 1, each extra machine adds less than the one before.
3
The exponents are income shares. With competitive markets, labour earns 1−α of income and capital earns α.
Penn World Table via FRED — labour compensation as a share of output
The data say labour takes about 0.6, so α is nearer 0.4 than 0.3 — and the share has fallen six points since 2000.
  • α = 0.3 is the textbook’s round number. A fixed exponent cannot reproduce a drifting share — another reminder that this is a fit, not a description.
