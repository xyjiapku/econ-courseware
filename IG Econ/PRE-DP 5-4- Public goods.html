Public goods

Beyond the name: rivalry, excludability & real-world classifications

First test: rivalry

Does my consumption take something away from you?

Rival — one person’s use leaves less for everyone else. The slice you eat is gone.

Non-rival — an extra user takes nothing away. A radio broadcast, a lighthouse beam, a defence system: the same output serves one more person at no extra cost.

The test is about what is left after me, not about how many people want it.

Second test: excludability

Can non-payers be kept out — at a reasonable cost?

Excludable — a fence, a gate, a ticket can stop people who do not pay.

Non-excludable — once the good exists, nobody can be kept out. You cannot fence in the air over a city, or the light from a lighthouse, or the defence of a country.

The test is technical and cheap: a fence that costs more than the crop it protects is no exclusion at all.

Named “public” — actually club goods

Excludable + non-rival (within capacity): a fee keeps non-payers out.

Public swimming pools, cinemas, bridges

A ticket or toll excludes non-payers; when uncrowded one more user costs almost nothing.

Public libraries

A library card is required, but enough stock keeps reading largely non-rival.

Cable / pay TV

The encrypted signal excludes; an extra subscriber adds almost zero cost.

Toll roads (uncrowded)

The booth excludes free riders; light traffic means low rivalry.

Named “public” — actually common resources

Non-excludable + rival: hard to keep people out, and one person’s use reduces what is left.

Public fisheries, pastures

Anyone may fish or graze; the fish and grass are limited, so overuse depletes them.

Public ponds and rivers

Villagers draw water freely; too much extraction lowers the water for everyone.

Congested public roads

Open access, but once crowded each extra car slows the rest.

Clean air

Hard to exclude polluters; beyond the air’s capacity it becomes scarce and rival.

Named “public” — actually private goods

Excludable + rival: labelled public, consumed like anything else.

Public buses, public toilets

A seat or stall used by one person cannot be used by another at the same time; a fare excludes.

Public phone booths

A coin is required, and only one person can use the phone.

Public housing

Once allocated, others are excluded; every unit given out reduces the stock.

The free-rider problem

Why a firm will not build a lighthouse, however useful it is.

Non-excludable → you cannot charge. Once the light is on, every ship sees it — including the ones that paid nothing.

Free rider → nobody pays. Each owner waits for someone else to pay, because they get the light either way.

No revenue → no supply. A private firm cannot cover its cost, so the good is not produced at all — not even a little.

The market does not under-produce a pure public good — it produces none

So how is it provided?

If the price cannot do it, something else has to.

Tax and provide

The state pays out of taxation and provides it free at the point of use — defence, street lighting, flood defences.

Make it excludable

Turn it into a club good: a toll, a subscription, an encrypted signal. That is how pay TV and toll roads exist.

Regulate or subsidise

Require provision, or pay a private firm to supply it — road maintenance contracts, subsidies for basic research.

Each answer moves the good out of the bottom-right cell — or takes the decision away from the market entirely
