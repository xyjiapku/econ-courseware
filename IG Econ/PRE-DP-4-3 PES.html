THE FAMINE · 饥荒

Bad harvest. Empty shelves. Raise the price?

One region, one failed harvest, one hard decision — and two voices arguing.

The situation

The harvest fails. Grain is short: queues, empty shelves, anger. The government must decide — let the price rise, or cap it?

"Let it rise"

A higher price pulls out more supply — growers and traders ship grain in, so more people get food.

"Cap the price"

Cap it, or the poorest cannot eat. Hunger becomes a money problem, not a shortage.

Your call? Hold it — the famine comes back on the second-to-last case page, with today's tool in hand.

Voice A assumes supply can respond to price. Steep or flat — nobody has checked. That is the question of the day.

Voice B assumes the problem is money, not quantity. Also decided by the same number — and by what a price rise does to hoarding.

Both voices argue about the same thing: how much supply can answer a price. Today we measure it.

SAME PRICE RISE · 直觉

Same price rise — two very different answers

Price rises ¥30 → ¥36 (a fixed +20%). Watch how differently quantity supplied answers.

The experiment

Hold the price rise fixed (+20%) — the only thing that differs is the steepness of supply.

① Steep supply

Quantity 5 → 5.3: the same +20% price rise brings hardly any extra supply.

② Flat supply

Quantity 5 → 11: the same +20% price rise brings a lot of extra supply.

Same price rise, very different quantity answer. Everything else equal — the number that measures this gap is on the next page.

DEFINITION · 定义

Meet PES

We need a number that says how strongly sellers respond to a price change. Here it is.

1 · The idea — Coursebook: "PES measures the extent to which the quantity supplied changes when the price of a product changes."

2 · The formula

PES= %ΔQs%ΔP = ΔQ ÷ QΔP ÷ P

3 · Read the number. PES is positive — price up, quantity supplied up (the law of supply). A PES of 2.6 means a 1% price rise extends supply by 2.6%.

4 · Why bother? Because the same price rise can feed a region or just move money around (the famine), swing for years (pigs), and decide who pays a tax — last lesson. PES predicts which one happens.

CALCULATION · 计算

Work out a PES — step by step

Read the question. Work out your answer. Then reveal the four steps.

The question

Price rises from $10 to $12. Quantity supplied rises from 100 to 130.
Find the PES — and say whether supply is elastic.

1 · %ΔQs — change ÷ original × 100: (130 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = +30%

2 · %ΔP — same method: (12 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = +20%

3 · Divide — PES = +30% ÷ +20% = 1.5

4 · Read it. PES > 1 → elastic: a 1% price rise pulls out 1.5% more supply. Price falls? Both changes go negative — the ratio stays positive.

5 · Keep going — two more, straight from the coursebook: ① Q 10 000 → 4 000 when price $5 → $4. ② Q 200 → 210 when price $10 → $14. Work both, then reveal.

✓ ① 3 · elastic — −60% ÷ −20%; both fall, the ratio stays positive. ✓ ② 0.125 · inelastic — +5% ÷ +40%: price quadruples, supply barely moves.

PUZZLE · 猜想

Two lines through the origin — guess both PES values

Both supply curves start at the origin. Price rises from ¥1 to ¥5. What is the PES on each?

The set-up

S₁: P = Q (shallow). S₂: P = 2Q (twice as steep). Price ¥1 → ¥5 = +400%. Guess each PES first.

① On S₁ (P = Q)

Q goes 1 → 5, so %ΔQ = +400% → PES = 400% ÷ 400% = 1.

② On S₂ (P = 2Q)

Q goes 0.5 → 2.5 — also +400% → PES = 1 again, though the line is twice as steep.

The rule: any straight line through the origin has PES = 1 at every point. Steepness does not matter — only proportionality does. Proof below.

Why (four lines): P = k×Q → Q = P ÷ k → Q′ ÷ Q = P′ ÷ P (the k cancels) → PES = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP = 1. A line not through the origin has a different PES at each point.

FIVE DEGREES · 五种

Five degrees — each with its own curve

Read the shape first, then the number. The last one you just proved.

ELASTIC · PES > 1 — shallow line, touches the price axis. Paper clips, t-shirts.

INELASTIC · PES < 1 — steep line, touches the quantity axis. Wheat, apples, pork.

PERFECTLY INELASTIC · PES = 0 — vertical. Cinema seats tonight.

PERFECTLY ELASTIC · PES = ∞ — horizontal. One seller among many identical ones.

UNITARY · PES = 1 — any straight line through the origin (proved above).

DETERMINANT 1

The longer it takes to make, the LOWER the PES

Coursebook: "If it takes a long time to make a product, supply is likely to be inelastic."

The production period decides it

This is about the product itself: how long from "make more" to the goods arriving. Longer production period → lower PES.

ProductTime to make one moreSupply
Cups of coffeea minuteelastic
Chairsa few dayselastic
Pork≈ 10 months (sow → market pig)inelastic

Why the pig cycle exists

Pork cannot be rushed: sow → piglet 4 months, to 7 kg 1 month, to market weight 5 months. A price signal today becomes meat only 10 months later — so supply cannot answer quickly, and the market overshoots.

Exam line: long production period → inelastic. (This is not the same as "give firms more time" — that is the next page.)

DETERMINANT 3

Spare capacity — is there room to ramp up?

Coursebook: if most factors are already in use, supply cannot rise much.

Idle machines → ELASTIC

Machines used a few hours a day, workers on short shifts → a price rise just means switch them on.

Running flat out → INELASTIC

Every machine busy, every field planted → more output needs new capital and new land — that takes time.

Why farms lose here: land is always fully used. A factory can add a night shift; a wheat field cannot add a second summer.

Example — the same order, two factories

Factory A runs one shift → takes the order in days. Factory B runs three shifts → needs new machines, quoted in months.

Exam line: spare capacity → supply can extend quickly and cheaply → elastic.

DETERMINANT 4

Can it be stored? Stocks bend the curve

Coursebook: storable products can be released or held back when the price moves.

Storable → ELASTIC

Oil in tanks, grain in silos, tinned food: price rises → sell from stock; price falls → hold it back. No waiting for a harvest.

Perishable → INELASTIC

Fresh milk, strawberries, a haircut, a seat tonight: whatever is offered today is all there is.

Connect the dots: "bring grain in fast" works because someone, somewhere, stored it — and China's frozen-pork reserve is storage policy for the pig cycle.

The 2008 proof

World rice production in 2007/08 was a record high — yet prices tripled. Hoarding + export bans emptied the market, not the silos. When Japan released stock, prices fell at once.

Exam line: storable → stock smooths the response → elastic.

OTHER INFLUENCES · 因素

Remaining influences — one look

Coursebook factors, briefly. The first three have their own pages; these are the rest.

Time period

The longer producers are given, the more elastic: momentary → short run → long run. Farmers cannot replant tonight, but they can over years.

Cost of altering supply

Cheap to change output → elastic. Expensive — new plant, scarce specialists → inelastic. Software ≫ a steel mill.

Mobility of factors

Workers, land and capital that can switch to making the dearer good → elastic. Delivery riders switch overnight.

Natural resources

A resource that is being depleted gets harder to expand → inelastic. Fish stocks in decline.

Room ≠ cheap (don't mix these up). Spare capacity asks "is there room?" — idle machines switch on. Cost of altering supply asks "how expensive is the change?" — even with room, ramping up may need costly kit, so firms think twice.

WHY IT MATTERS · 意义

Two people who really need PES

One is deciding whether to expand. One is deciding who pays.

The firm 💼

Demand jumps — can you actually supply it? Elastic supply → take the sales. Inelastic → customers go elsewhere, or you pay overtime.

The government 🏛️

Incidence needs both elasticities. Elastic supply → firms escape a tax. Inelastic supply → firms cannot escape it.

Link back to last lesson: a tax lands on the less elastic side — of demand and of supply. Steep S + steep D → the two sides share the burden; flat S → consumers pay almost all.

And for a famine? If supply cannot respond in time, a higher price does not create more grain — it only moves money. Test that on the last case page.

REAL CASE · 猪周期

The pig cycle — China's supply-response lag

Real live-hog prices, 2019–2025. Demand barely moves — the supply response takes about ten months.

Live-hog price — six real turning points: ¥38.71 (Oct 2019) → ¥11.54 (Oct 2021) → ¥27.66 (Oct 2022) → ¥14.19 (Jul 2023) → ¥20.92 (Aug 2024) → ¥12.30 (Oct 2025). — a −70% fall in two years.

Source: China Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs monitoring, via a Huaan Securities industry report (Mar 2026).

① Why the wave

Price up → farmers keep more sows → ≈10 months later pigs flood the market → price down → sows cut → shortage again.

② The ten months

Sow → piglet 4 months; to 7 kg 1 month; to market weight 5 months ≈ 10 months from decision to meat.

③ The herd moves first

African swine fever: sow herd −32.9% (Dec 2017 → Sep 2019), then +52.1% by Jun 2021 — the herd swings before the price does.

1995–2022: seven complete cycles, shortest 36 months, longest 49 months → "3–4 years". Inelastic supply + a time lag = overshooting.

Historic pig cycles, 2006–2022 (monthly live-hog price). Source: Wind / Ministry of Agriculture / Zheshang Securities.

POLICY · 平滑周期

Fighting a lag you cannot remove

Nobody can make a pig grow faster. So policy works on the information and the stock.

Buy low, release high

State frozen-pork reserves: the government buys when price is low, sells when it is high — the missing stock that private farms cannot hold.

Target the herd, not the price

A sow-inventory band (normal ≈ 43 m sows, floor 40 m) warns farmers before they all expand together.

Contracts and futures

Hog futures let farms lock a price today — less guesswork, less herd-and-slaughter.

Information

Publish the sow count and expected supply — if everyone knows the coming flood, fewer join it.

Why it works: the lag (≈10 months) cannot be removed, so the aim is to stop everyone reacting at once — smoothing, not eliminating, the cycle.

BACK TO THE FAMINE · 回答

So — should the price have been capped?

Build it: the market, then the shortage, then the grain that arrives from elsewhere.

⓪ Before the famine

Grain: D and S cross at ¥30 / 5 units. Supply is steep — food cannot be made overnight.

① The harvest fails → D jumps

Price ¥30 → ¥34.8 (+16%) but food supplied only 5 → 5.2 (+5%): PES ≈ 0.3.

The price rise moves money, it does not grow grain. And a bigger rise invites hoarding and panic buying.

② Grain arrives from elsewhere

Imports at ¥33 → total food 5.2 → 5.6 and the price falls back to ¥33. More food, lower price — from moving grain, not growing it.

The answer: let the price rise some — but cap the extremes, stop hoarding, and bring grain in fast.

A WAY OF THINKING

Three ways to see the same famine

See the mountain as a mountain → see it as not a mountain → see it as a mountain again.
1

No economics — the moral answer

"Prices must not rise — the poor would starve." Intuition and fairness. It protects people, but it cannot say what the price rise would actually do.

2

A little economics — the clever answer

"Let prices rise! A higher price calls out more supply." Simple, confident — and it sounds anti-moral. It treats supply as if it always answers.

3

Deeper economics — the full answer

Short-run PES ≈ 0, and high prices invite hoarding. So: allow some rise, cap the extremes, stop hoarding, import fast. The moral goal of stage 1 survives — now with science under it.

A caution. Some popular writers — Xue Zhaofeng (薛兆丰) is the classic case — like using first-pass economics to dress up anti-common-sense, anti-moral conclusions.

Economics is first a tool for understanding reality and the science behind common sense. Being counter-intuitive can sharpen thinking; it is not a licence for being counter-moral.
