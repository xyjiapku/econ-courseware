The balance sheet

Every argument on the left has a matching cost on the right.

What the mix adds

  • Public and merit goods. No firm would supply defence to everyone.
  • External costs are curbed. The polluter pays, so less is produced.
  • Equality. Taxes and benefits reach those the market left out.
  • Choice survives. Most goods are still made by competing private firms.
  • Stability. Spending holds demand up in a slump.

What it costs

  • Government failure. The cure can waste more than the disease.
  • Higher taxes. Every service is paid for, and taxes weaken incentives.
  • Bureaucracy. No profit test, no rival, no pressure to improve.
  • Less innovation. Regulation lowers the reward for risk.
  • Enforcement. Rules cost money to police and can be captured.
So the exam question is never “is the state good?” — it is which intervention, here, at what cost

Evaluating = weighing cost against benefit

Not “is the state good?” but “is this worth what it costs?”

1 · Name the failure — what is being corrected, and how.

2 · List the cost — money, administration, and the opportunity cost.

3 · List the benefit — health, output, time, and who receives it.

4 · Compare, then conclude — “worth it if…”, naming the condition.

Cost-benefit analysis is this comparison written down.

Cost-benefit in practice: coal-to-gas in rural Hebei

Ask the class first: what is gained, what is paid? Then weigh it.
Gas meters on a village wall, Hebei.

COST

  • Gas costs more than coal
  • Pipes and meters to be paid for
  • Cold snap, short supply: homes go cold

BENEFIT

  • Cleaner air, less smoke
  • Fewer chest and heart illnesses
  • A city visitors want to come to

Worth it if the health gain is large and the gas supply holds — it can lose on both counts.

Two ways to cap a quantity

Both fix how much is allowed. Only one lets the right to do it be bought and sold.
QUOTA

A limit set by the government on the quantity of a product that can be supplied, extracted or imported.

TRADEABLE PERMIT

A permit allowing a firm to emit up to a stated amount, which firms may buy and sell; the permits issued add up to a cap.

Resource extraction

rare earths · fish · timber

Pollution

SO₂ · waste water · industrial emissions

Carbon

EU ETS 2005 · China’s national market 2021

Quota = a number nobody may exceed. Permit = the same number, plus a market that moves it to the cheapest user

Two firms, one cap

The cap is shared out. Then the two firms discover they are not equally good at cutting.

What a permit is worth

The price is the whole policy: it tells every firm, without a regulator visiting, how much its pollution costs.

EU allowance, € a tonne: about 15 in 2018, near 85 at the 2022–23 peak, around 65 today.

China’s national market trades far lower — roughly ¥70–100 a tonne — which is why economists argue the cap is still loose.

Read the line, not the words: when the price rises, every firm re-checks whether polluting is still cheaper than cleaning up.

A cap nobody trades is just a rule; a cap with a price becomes a decision every firm makes for itself
