Information failure in your life

Three things you have probably done this year.

1 · Bought on 拼多多

Was the price low because it was cheap to make — or because you could not check what you were buying?

2 · Sold on 闲鱼

Who knew more about the thing you sold — you, or the person buying it?

3 · A second-hand laptop

Would you pay the “fair” price if the seller knew the battery was dying and you did not?

A second-hand market: one side always knows more.

Every one of these is a market with a gap in the information. Today we look at what that gap does to the price.

Information failure

When buyers or sellers lack the information needed to make the best economic decisions.

Three types

1 · Lack of information

the fact exists somewhere; you do not have it

2 · Misleading information

the fact you have is wrong — often because someone chose to make it wrong

3 · Asymmetric information

one party knows more than the other — and the price is set as if they knew the same

An exam you cannot revise for — that is what a market feels like when the information is missing.

All three end the same way: resources go to the wrong place, and we call that market failure.

Two failures, two fixes

The same two problems you meet every week — and what policy does about each.

Lack of information

A second-hand phone

You cannot open it. The seller knows the battery is dying; you find out in March.

A new restaurant

You cannot taste it before you pay. Its kitchen is invisible until someone inspects it.

The fix. Make information flow: ratings and review platforms, certification marks, mandatory labelling, third-party inspection, the 12315 complaint system.

Misleading information

A “zero sugar” drink

The claim is legal and the sugar is replaced by something you were not told about.

Fake reviews and scam adverts

Paid five-star ratings, “miracle cures” aimed at the elderly, phishing messages that look official.

The fix. Punish the lie: advertising law, fines for false claims, platform liability for what it carries, anti-fraud alerts and take-down power.
One is an empty signal, the other a false one — and both make the price say something untrue

Merit and demerit goods

Two definitions, both with two halves.

Merit goods

Goods that are more beneficial to consumers than they realise, and which also create external benefits.

education · vaccinations · health insurance

Demerit goods

Goods that are more harmful than consumers realise, and which also create external costs.

cigarettes · alcohol · gambling

Both halves matter: the misjudgement is information failure; the spillover is an externality. Left alone, the market under-produces the first and over-produces the second.

Merit and demerit goods — two ingredients

① Information failure

the buyer does not know the benefit, or discounts it because it arrives years from now

② External benefits

other people gain when this is consumed — and they never paid for it

Take either one away and it is no longer a merit good. Without the externality it is just a good people misjudge; without the misjudgement it is just a good with an externality.

A village wall: finish middle school before going out to work — education is the clearest merit good

Merit good: health insurance

Dying to Survive (2018)

Why demand is too low. Insurers cannot tell the healthy from the sick, so they charge one price — the sick buy, the young and healthy stay out (adverse selection). Once insured, people use more care than they would have (moral hazard).

External benefits. Timely treatment lowers medical costs for everyone and lowers the risk of disease spreading — the neighbours gain and never pay.

The film. Imported cancer drugs priced beyond what families can pay, and the grey market that grew to fill the gap: the medicine was a merit good, and the price kept it from the people who needed it.

The American case

No universal public cover — insurance is bought from firms that must make a profit.
The 2010 attempt to widen cover — still fought over in court and at every election.
December 2024: a health-insurance chief executive was shot dead in Manhattan.

A profit-making insurer earns most by covering the healthy and refusing the expensive. The poor end up under-insured, then face the bill alone — one illness can erase a family’s savings. The anger is the political symptom of an economic gap.

A merit good left to the market is under-consumed — and when it is also expensive, the shortfall becomes a social conflict, not just an inefficient one

Demerit goods — the mirror image

Harm under-estimated, and shared.
One warns: the harm is real, and it is not only yours.
One sells: the same product, dressed as a gift.

Same good, two messages. A demerit good is one where the second message wins — and the buyer pays for the first one later.

China, 2019–2025, trillion yuan. Tobacco tax and profit is about the size of the whole defence budget — and total health spending is more than five times either.

Tobacco brings in 1.60 trillion yuan a year — and costs more than that to clean up after. Published estimates of the social cost range from 0.9 to 2.4 trillion yuan, and every one is larger than the revenue. Roughly, one yuan of tobacco tax comes with 1.6 yuan of social cost.

Tax is a policy choice

VAT is the floor every good pays. The choice is what a government adds on top — or takes off.
9%

Reduced rate

Fresh food — agricultural products sit in the reduced VAT band.

Books, newspapers, magazines — also 9%, not the standard 13%.

Made cheaper on purpose: the benefit is private and social.
13%

Standard rate, nothing extra

Everyday packaged goods — bottled water, snacks, clothing — pay 13% and nothing more.

The default setting: neither encouraged nor discouraged.
13% + excise

Standard rate + excise

Tobacco — 36–56% excise + 11% wholesale

Alcohol — 20% excise + a fixed charge per unit

Sugar-sweetened drinks — proposed long debated, never adopted

In force for tobacco and alcohol; only a paper for sugar.
Same instrument, three settings: a reduced rate encourages, the standard rate is neutral, an excise discourages
