Two roads out of the plan

China was not the only one — from the mid-1980s, planned economies let prices and private firms back in.
1979, and the slogan that carried it: “keep the line for a hundred years”
1

Let prices work

Vietnam’s Doi Moi (1986) ended collective farming and let farmers sell at market prices.

2

Allow private firms

India’s 1991 reform dismantled the licence system that told firms what they could produce.

3

Open to trade

Poland freed prices in 1990 and privatised state industry; Eastern Europe moved in months.

4

But not wholesale

The state kept schools, health and the utilities — which is what made the result mixed.

And the other road: the West built a welfare state

One side left the plan, the other built a public sector — both ended up mixed

Three definitions, word for word

The mark is in who owns and controls — and in both.

Mixed economic system — an economy that includes features of both a planned and a market system: some firms are privately owned, and some are owned by the state.

Public sector

The part of the economy owned and controlled by the government; decisions are taken by public bodies, not in pursuit of profit.

Private sector

The part of the economy owned and controlled by individuals and firms; decisions are taken in pursuit of profit.

Worked example 1: a polluting factory

Private cost is below social cost. The firm over-produces; the river pays.
The cost exists — it is just not in the price.
1

Tax the discharge

A charge per unit lifts private cost towards social cost.

2

Regulate the limit

A cap, monitored and fined, fixes the damage directly.

3

Subsidise the fix

Pay part of the cleaner equipment, so abating is cheaper than polluting.

4

Publish the data

Buyers and neighbours change behaviour faster than a regulator can.

Worked example 2: education

A merit good — and the clearest case for supplying rather than nudging.
1

Direct provision

The state builds and runs schools and pays the teachers. Families pay nothing at the gate — a price would keep some out.

2

Subsidy

Free meals, textbooks, transport — lowering the cost that stops attendance.

3

Regulation

A school-leaving age, a national curriculum, qualified teachers.

4

Information

Tell families what the returns to schooling are — the misjudgement is half the failure.

A village wall: finish middle school before going out to work.
REASON 2

Because allocative efficiency is not the only goal

A market can be efficient and still produce a society nobody wants.
1

Equity

An efficient allocation can leave a child hungry. Redistribution is a judgement about fairness, not a correction of inefficiency.

2

Stability

Boom and bust is not the market clearing — it is the market hurting most those least able to carry it.

3

Security

Food, energy, defence: some things a country will not leave to whoever is cheapest.

4

Political stability

A society that leaves whole regions out does not stay peaceful. Intervention buys legitimacy as well as output.

So intervention is not a second best wheeled in when markets fail — it is a chosen part of the mix
