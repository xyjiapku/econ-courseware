Microeconomics and macroeconomics

Same economy, two questions.

Microeconomics

The study of individual markets.

One household, one firm, one price: what to buy, what to produce, what to charge.

Macroeconomics

The study of the economy as a whole.

Total output, total employment, the price level — and what government spending and taxation do to them.

One person’s spending is another person’s income

The classical answer: supply creates its own demand

Run it. Nothing should go wrong — that was the claim.
Say’s Law: producing something creates exactly the income to buy it back

Marx: the circle has an exit

Run it again — this time profit leaves the loop and does not all return.
The crisis is not a malfunction — it is the system working as designed
Karl Marx · 1818–1883

In this sense, the theory of the Communists may be summed up in the single sentence: abolition of private property.

The Communist Manifesto, 1848

If ownership causes the crisis, the cure is to change who owns.

They tried it — and it failed differently

Abolishing private property removed the crisis. It replaced it with something worse.
1991: the statues came down before the shortage did.
1

No prices, no signal

Nothing tells a planner that steel is piling up and shoes are short.

2

Quotas, not wants

Output is counted in tonnes, so tonnes get made — wanted or not.

3

No reward for doing it better

No profit, no competition, no reason to improve.

4

The result

Shortages, queues, waste — and eventually collapse.

Keynes: the circle can be restarted

Same model, one new actor — the government. Watch what the injection does.
Private ownership survives; the public sector is born as a stabiliser

What Keynes proposed

If the private sector will not spend, the state spends for it — and the circle turns again.

Borrow if you must: a deficit is spending today that a growing economy pays back tomorrow. And do not wait for wages to fall — “in the long run we are all dead.”

Two roads out of the plan

China was not the only one — from the mid-1980s, planned economies let prices and private firms back in.
1979, and the slogan that carried it: keep the line for a hundred years.
1

Let prices work

Vietnam’s Doi Moi (1986) ended collective farming and let farmers sell at market prices.

2

Allow private firms

India’s 1991 reform dismantled the licence system that told firms what they could produce.

3

Open to trade

Poland freed prices in 1990 and privatised state industry; Eastern Europe moved in months.

4

But not wholesale

The state kept schools, health and the utilities — which is what made the result mixed.

And the other road: the West built a welfare state

The same decades, the opposite direction — market economies adding a public sector.
The New Deal, 1933 · and the European poster tradition it fed
1

United States · 1933

Roosevelt’s New Deal: public works, bank regulation, and social insurance in 1935.

2

Britain · 1945

The Labour government nationalised coal, rail and steel, and founded the NHS in 1948 — health free at the point of use.

3

France · 1945–75

Banks and energy nationalised, and a national plan set investment targets for industry.

4

Scandinavia

Social democracy: high taxes, universal benefits, active labour-market policy.

One side left the plan, the other built a public sector — both ended up mixed

Three definitions, word for word

The mark is in who owns and controls — and in both.

Mixed economic system — an economy that includes features of both a planned and a market system: some firms are privately owned, and some are owned by the state.

Public sector

The part of the economy owned and controlled by the government; decisions are taken by public bodies, not in pursuit of profit.

Private sector

The part of the economy owned and controlled by individuals and firms; decisions are taken in pursuit of profit.

REASON 1

To correct market failure

The four types you met in 2.9 — none of them self-corrects.

Every one has the same shape: the price does not carry the whole story.

Someone outside the transaction gains or loses, and nobody pays.

Left alone, none of them corrects itself.

THE TOOLKIT

Nine instruments — the complete list

Everything a government can do about market failure. From the next page, we start on the examples.
1 · TaxRaises the price of what is over-produced.
2 · SubsidyLowers the price of what is under-produced.
3 · RegulationForbids or requires: standards, limits.
4 · QuotaCaps the quantity that may be supplied or extracted.
5 · Max / min priceOverrides the price the market would set.
6 · Direct provisionThe state supplies it: rail, grid, water.
7 · InformationCorrects the misjudgement: labels, warnings.
8 · Competition policyRestrains monopoly; opens the market.
9 · Tradeable permitsCreates a market in the right to pollute — taken last, in lesson 3.
Why the ninth is left to the end: it only makes sense once you have seen the other eight fail to be efficient

Worked example 1: a polluting factory

Private cost is below social cost. The firm over-produces; the river pays.
The cost exists — it is just not in the price.
1

Tax the discharge

A charge per unit lifts private cost towards social cost.

2

Regulate the limit

A cap, monitored and fined, fixes the damage directly.

3

Subsidise the fix

Pay part of the cleaner equipment, so abating is cheaper than polluting.

4

Publish the data

Buyers and neighbours change behaviour faster than a regulator can.

Worked example 2: education

A merit good — and the clearest case for supplying rather than nudging.
1

Direct provision

The state builds and runs schools and pays the teachers. Families pay nothing at the gate — a price would keep some out.

2

Subsidy

Free meals, textbooks, transport — lowering the cost that stops attendance.

3

Regulation

A school-leaving age, a national curriculum, qualified teachers.

4

Information

Tell families what the returns to schooling are — the misjudgement is half the failure.

A village wall: finish middle school before going out to work.
REASON 2

Because allocative efficiency is not the only goal

A market can be efficient and still produce a society nobody wants.
1

Equity

An efficient allocation can leave a child hungry. Redistribution is a judgement about fairness, not a correction of inefficiency.

2

Stability

Boom and bust is not the market clearing — it is the market hurting most those least able to carry it.

3

Security

Food, energy, defence: some things a country will not leave to whoever is cheapest.

4

Political stability

A society that leaves whole regions out does not stay peaceful. Intervention buys legitimacy as well as output.

So intervention is not a second best wheeled in when markets fail — it is a chosen part of the mix
