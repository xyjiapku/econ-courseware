Competition policy

Government action to keep markets competitive — and to stop a firm using the power it already has.

Competition policy — the laws and regulators a government uses to prevent firms from restricting competition: stopping them fixing prices, abusing a dominant position, or merging into one.

Prohibit

Exclusive dealing, price fixing, predatory pricing.

Block mergers

Two large firms must not become one that can do as it likes.

Break up

When the position is already entrenched.

Open the market

Imports, a second licence.

The trusts, drawn in 1900: one rule for everyone else.

2026: the Ctrip case

How a firm becomes dominant, what it does with that power, and what the regulator did about it.
56%bookings, 2024
~70%with Tongcheng
32%net margin

That share let it set the terms every hotel had to accept:

1. Get on or get out. Top-badged hotels sold only on Ctrip.

2. Lowest price on the web. Everyone else had to match anyone, enforced by software that cut prices automatically.

3. Penalties. Less traffic, loss of the badge, deducted deposits.

Fine: ¥5.18 bn in July 2026 — 7.5% of sales, after a six-month investigation.

Blocking a merger

A merger removes a rival without removing any cost — the cheapest thing a regulator can refuse.
AT&T and T-Mobile USA, 2011: $39 bn, refused.

What was proposed. In 2011 AT&T, America’s second-largest carrier, agreed to buy T-Mobile USA for $39 bn — turning four national networks into three.

Why it was stopped. The antitrust review found the deal would raise prices for millions of subscribers, and that no efficiency gain could offset the loss of a rival.

What it cost to stop. Nothing but a refusal — no fine, no break-up order, no compensation.

Contrast Ctrip: cleared to merge in 2015, and fined in 2026 for the dominance that merger helped create.

1911: the Standard Oil break-up

The case that made “break it up” a real option. Click the chart to enlarge it.
One company, 34 successors — click to enlarge.
90%US refining, 1904
34companies after 1911

Standard Oil controlled refining, pipelines and rail rebates; rivals could not reach the market on equal terms.

The Sherman Act (1890) made that illegal. In 1911 the Supreme Court ordered the company dissolved.

Exxon, Mobil, Chevron, Amoco came out of it — competition returned by ending the monopoly.

Where competition would be wasteful

Not that two firms could not run a network — duplicating it would halve the use.

The grid. Generation can be private and competitive; transmission is one natural monopoly.

The track. One asset, many trains — the state owns the network and licenses operators.

Water. One set of pipes under every street, so the supplier is a public utility.

Competition policy has nothing to split here — the network itself is the monopoly. So: regulate the price, or own it.

But a state firm can be inefficient

The absence of a rival removes the pressure that keeps any firm sharp.
1

No rival, no benchmark

With nothing to compare against, waste is hard even to see.

2

Soft budget

Losses are covered by the state, so carelessness is not paid for by the firm.

3

Many masters

Employment, price stability and regional policy may outrank efficiency.

4

Weak incentives

No profit share, no share price, no takeover — less reason to improve.

Privatisation

Privatisation — the sale of state-owned firms or assets to the private sector.

Britain from 1984: telecom, gas, water, the railways. China from the 1990s: small state firms sold, the largest kept.

Why sell? To raise money, to bring in competition, and to put the firm under the discipline of a share price.

Courier services opened to private firms — the privatisation that built e-commerce.

Nationalisation

Nationalisation — a government taking a private sector industry into state ownership.

1

Britain, 1945–51

The Attlee government took coal, the railways, steel and the hospitals into public ownership — the NHS began in 1948.

2

France, 1945

Banks, coal and electricity were nationalised, and a national plan set investment targets for industry.

3

Why take it?

Private owners would not rebuild after the war; the industry served the country, not a shareholder.

Britain nationalised the railways in 1948 and privatised them again in the 1990s

Price controls

A legal price the market may not reach — so demand and supply no longer meet.

Maximum price — below equilibrium → shortage (rent control)

Minimum price — above equilibrium → surplus (minimum wage)

Maximum price in practice: rent control

The ceiling protects the tenant inside the flat — and shrinks the supply of flats for everyone after them.

Paris — the ceiling bites. Rent is capped, so building becomes unprofitable and landlords let flats decay. Supply grows more slowly than demand: the shortage is managed, not removed.

China, land reform — the ceiling does nothing. “Reduce rent and interest” created no shortage, because the supply of farmland is fixed by nature, not by the return on it.

The difference is not the policy — it is whether supply responds to price.

Minimum price in practice: the minimum wage

The textbook says a floor creates a surplus of workers. The evidence says the surplus can disappear — and that is what the Nobel was for.
Wages rise → productivity rises → demand for labour shifts to D′; at Pmin the two curves meet.

The natural experiment. New Jersey raised its minimum wage in 1992; neighbouring Pennsylvania did not. Card and Krueger compared fast-food jobs on both sides of the border.

The result. Employment in New Jersey did not fall relative to Pennsylvania — the surplus never showed up.

The prize. David Card shared the 2021 Nobel for it: a natural experiment answered a question the model alone could not.

So the exam answer is conditional — a large rise, with elastic demand, still costs jobs.
